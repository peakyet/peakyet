Quasi-Newton Methods

Newton's method knows how the landscape bends, but measuring the full Hessian can cost more than taking the step. Quasi-Newton methods replace that measurement with a running experiment: move, observe how the gradient changed, and update a curvature model that agrees with what was just seen. BFGS is the most successful version of that idea.

1. The expensive question: which way is really downhill?

Suppose we minimize a smooth function in a long, tilted valley. The negative gradient points downhill, but it ignores the valley's shape. It repeatedly crosses the narrow direction and makes slow progress along the floor. Newton's method fixes this by rescaling and rotating the gradient with the inverse Hessian:

\[p_k^{\text{Newton}}=-\nabla^2 f(x_k)^{-1}\nabla f(x_k).\]

That direction is excellent when the Hessian is available and cheap. In a problem with millions of variables, however, a dense Hessian contains roughly half a trillion distinct entries. Even for moderate dimensions, deriving, assembling, factoring, and debugging second derivatives may dominate the optimization.

Intuition: a Hessian is a local gearbox. It says how to convert “the gradient is this large” into “move this far in this direction.” Quasi-Newton methods learn the gearbox while driving.
Takeaway: quasi-Newton methods aim for Newton-like directions using gradients, not explicit second derivatives.

2. A curvature measurement hidden in two gradients

Take a step from \(x_k\) to \(x_{k+1}\). Record the displacement and the gradient change:

\[s_k=x_{k+1}-x_k,\qquad y_k=\nabla f(x_{k+1})-\nabla f(x_k).\]

For a quadratic function with Hessian \(A\), gradients are affine, so this measurement is exact:

\[y_k=A s_k.\]

For a general smooth function, the fundamental theorem of calculus gives the precise version:

\[y_k=\left(\int_0^1 \nabla^2 f(x_k+t s_k)\,dt\right)s_k.\]

The gradient difference is therefore the action of the average Hessian along the step. We did not measure every entry of that matrix, but we learned exactly what it does to one vector.

If \(B_{k+1}\) approximates the Hessian, consistency with this experiment requires the secant equation \(B_{k+1}s_k=y_k\). If \(H_{k+1}\approx B_{k+1}^{-1}\), the same requirement is

\[H_{k+1}y_k=s_k.\]
Why “secant”? In one dimension, Newton uses the tangent slope \(f''(x)\). Replacing it with the change in \(f'\) divided by the change in \(x\) gives a secant slope. The matrix equation above is the multidimensional version.
Takeaway: every accepted step yields a free directional curvature measurement, \(s_k\mapsto y_k\).

3. One observation is not a whole Hessian

A symmetric \(n\times n\) matrix has \(n(n+1)/2\) independent entries. The secant equation provides only \(n\) scalar constraints. Infinitely many symmetric matrices can satisfy it, so an update needs design principles beyond mere consistency.

  1. Keep symmetry. True Hessians of smooth scalar functions are symmetric.
  2. Preserve positive definiteness when the evidence supports it. Then \(-H_k g_k\) remains a descent direction.
  3. Change the old model as little as possible. The newest observation should correct the model, not erase all previous information.

BFGS is the rank-two update obtained from an appropriate weighted minimum-change problem subject to symmetry and the inverse secant equation.

Predict: after one step in a 100-variable problem, has BFGS “learned the Hessian”?

No. It has learned the Hessian's action along the latest displacement and retained its prior model elsewhere. The accumulation of informative directions—not one magical update—is what makes the model useful.

Takeaway: the secant equation narrows the choices; symmetry, positive definiteness, and minimum change select a useful update.

4. The BFGS update, read as two corrections

Let \(H_k\) be the current approximation to the inverse Hessian and define \(\rho_k=(y_k^\top s_k)^{-1}\). The inverse-BFGS update is

\[H_{k+1}=\left(I-\rho_k s_k y_k^\top\right)H_k\left(I-\rho_k y_k s_k^\top\right)+\rho_k s_k s_k^\top.\]

The formula looks elaborate, but its jobs are simple:

  • It removes the part of the old model that conflicts with the new secant observation.
  • It adds the observed inverse-curvature action along \(y_k\).
  • It changes the matrix by rank two, so the update costs \(O(n^2)\), not a new Hessian factorization.

Multiplying the formula by \(y_k\) immediately gives \(H_{k+1}y_k=s_k\). More importantly, if \(H_k\) is positive definite and

\[s_k^\top y_k>0,\]

then \(H_{k+1}\) is positive definite too. This scalar is the curvature condition. A Wolfe line search is valuable not merely because it decreases the objective; its curvature condition also makes \(s_k^\top y_k>0\) for a descent direction.

Predict: is “the objective decreased” enough to guarantee a safe BFGS update?

No. Sufficient decrease controls function values, but positive definiteness depends on the change in directional derivative. A curvature-aware line search supplies the missing condition.

The equivalent direct-Hessian update is useful when a solver needs \(B_k\) itself:

\[B_{k+1}=B_k-\frac{B_k s_k s_k^\top B_k}{s_k^\top B_k s_k}+\frac{y_k y_k^\top}{y_k^\top s_k}.\]
Notation warning: books disagree about whether \(H\) names the Hessian or its inverse. This note always uses \(B\approx\nabla^2 f\) and \(H\approx(\nabla^2 f)^{-1}\). Check the secant equation to see which convention a source uses.
Takeaway: BFGS is a cheap rank-two correction that enforces the latest curvature measurement while preserving a usable descent geometry.

5. Worked example: learning a tilted quadratic in two steps

Use the positive-definite quadratic

\[f(x)=\tfrac12x^\top\!\begin{bmatrix}4&1\\1&2\end{bmatrix}x-\begin{bmatrix}1\\1\end{bmatrix}^{\!\top}x,\qquad x_0=\begin{bmatrix}2\\-1\end{bmatrix}.\]

Its true Hessian is \(A\), but pretend we do not know that. Start with \(H_0=I\), so the first direction is simply steepest descent:

\[g_0=Ax_0-b=\begin{bmatrix}6\\-1\end{bmatrix},\qquad p_0=-H_0g_0=\begin{bmatrix}-6\\1\end{bmatrix}.\]

An exact line search gives \(\alpha_0=37/134\), followed by

\[x_1=\begin{bmatrix}23/67\\-97/134\end{bmatrix}\approx\begin{bmatrix}0.3433\\-0.7239\end{bmatrix}.\]

The measured pair is

\[s_0=\begin{bmatrix}-111/67\\37/134\end{bmatrix},\qquad y_0=\begin{bmatrix}-851/134\\-74/67\end{bmatrix},\qquad s_0^\top y_0=\frac{1369}{134}>0.\]

After the BFGS update, \(H_1y_0=s_0\) exactly. The second BFGS direction is no longer the negative gradient: it has been rotated and rescaled by the curvature learned on the first step. The second exact line search uses \(\alpha_1=134/259\) and lands at

\[x_2=\begin{bmatrix}1/7\\3/7\end{bmatrix}=x^\star,\qquad f(x_2)=-\frac27.\]

After that second independent curvature observation,

\[H_2=\begin{bmatrix}2/7&-1/7\\-1/7&4/7\end{bmatrix}=A^{-1}.\]

Predict: does BFGS always solve an \(n\)-variable problem in at most \(n\) steps?

Only in the ideal quadratic story (with exact arithmetic and exact line searches). On a nonlinear objective, the Hessian changes as we move; with inexact searches, noisy gradients, or rounding, the clean finite-termination argument no longer applies.

Takeaway: on a quadratic, each independent step teaches one exact curvature direction; in two dimensions, two such lessons can recover the entire inverse Hessian.

7. The practical algorithm: the line search is part of BFGS

given x, gradient tolerance ε, and H = I
while ||g(x)|| > ε:
    p = -H g(x)
    choose α satisfying strong Wolfe conditions
    s = α p
    y = g(x + s) - g(x)
    if sᵀy is safely positive:
        ρ = 1 / (sᵀy)
        H = (I - ρ s yᵀ) H (I - ρ y sᵀ) + ρ s sᵀ
    else:
        skip or damp the update
    x = x + s

What robust implementations pay attention to

  • Line search: Armijo decrease alone is not enough; a Wolfe curvature condition protects the update.
  • Initial scale: after the first pair, a common scalar model is \(H_0=\gamma I\) with \(\gamma=(s^\top y)/(y^\top y)\).
  • Curvature safeguard: if \(s^\top y\) is nonpositive or tiny relative to \(\lVert s\rVert\lVert y\rVert\), skip or damp rather than divide by an unreliable number.
  • Stopping: use a gradient norm, and often also limits on iterations, function evaluations, or step size.
  • Gradient quality: finite-difference noise can corrupt \(y_k\), precisely the quantity carrying the curvature information.
Debugging rule: log \(f_k\), \(\lVert g_k\rVert\), \(\alpha_k\), and \(s_k^\top y_k\). A stalled line search or repeatedly rejected curvature pairs usually explains a BFGS run long before inspecting the full matrix does.
Takeaway: BFGS is not just a matrix formula; it is the update, a curvature-aware line search, and numerical safeguards working together.

L-BFGS does not store a truncated matrix. It stores a short history of curvature observations and replays their algebra when a direction is needed. With \(m\) typically much smaller than \(n\), that is the difference between a method for thousands of variables and one for millions.

Dense BFGS
Stores \(n^2\) numbers and applies a dense matrix to each gradient. Best when dimensions are moderate and rich curvature memory is valuable.
L-BFGS
Stores about \(2mn\) numbers for the last \(m\) pairs. Best when the dimension makes a dense matrix impossible.
Takeaway: BFGS is the robust dense default; L-BFGS keeps the same secant logic while replacing the matrix with a short memory of observations.

9. Guarantees, failure modes, and where to go next

What the theory really promises

  • On a strictly convex quadratic with exact line searches, BFGS directions match conjugate-gradient structure and terminate in at most \(n\) steps in exact arithmetic.
  • Near a smooth minimizer, suitable line searches and regularity conditions can give superlinear convergence: the error shrinks faster than any fixed linear ratio.
  • Superlinear convergence does not require every entry of \(B_k\) to converge to the exact Hessian; the approximation must become accurate in the directions that matter to the steps.

When the mental model breaks

  • Nonconvex curvature: standard BFGS deliberately preserves a positive-definite model, so it does not directly represent negative curvature.
  • Noisy or nonsmooth gradients: the difference \(y_k\) may reflect noise or a kink rather than stable curvature.
  • Poor scaling: variables measured in wildly different units can waste many iterations before the model adapts.
  • Weak line searches: tiny steps give weak curvature information; failed curvature conditions force skipped or damped updates.
