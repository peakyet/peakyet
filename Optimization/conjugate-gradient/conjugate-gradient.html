Conjugate Gradients from Zero

A 2×2 system you can check by hand: what a descent direction has to satisfy, how to build one from residuals alone, and where the guarantee ends.

1. Two unknowns, two steps — what are the directions missing?

Question: this problem has only two unknowns. What is the largest number of steps any descent method with an exact line search should ever need on it, and what property must two successive directions have for that count to be reachable? State the property as one equation in \(p_0\) and \(p_1\), then say what that equation means when you read it through the matrix \(A\) rather than through the usual dot product.

Running example. We solve

\[ A=\begin{bmatrix}3&1\\1&2\end{bmatrix},\qquad b=\begin{bmatrix}1\\2\end{bmatrix},\qquad x_0=\begin{bmatrix}0\\0\end{bmatrix}. \]

\(A\) is symmetric and positive definite, so this is the same problem as minimising the strictly convex quadratic

\[ f(x)=\tfrac12 x^{\mathsf T}Ax-b^{\mathsf T}x,\qquad \nabla f(x)=Ax-b, \]

and \(f\) has exactly one minimiser. Substituting \(x^\star=(0,1)^{\mathsf T}\) confirms \(Ax^\star=b\), so the target is known in advance and every iterate can be checked against it. The level sets of \(f\) are the ellipses of Figure 1: the entire geometry a descent method moves in is carried by \(A\).

A descent method picks a direction \(p_k\) and a step length. For a quadratic the step length that makes \(f\) smallest along the line \(x_k+\alpha p_k\) is available in closed form:

\[ \alpha_k=\frac{r_k^{\mathsf T}p_k}{p_k^{\mathsf T}Ap_k},\qquad x_{k+1}=x_k+\alpha_k p_k,\qquad r_k=b-Ax_k. \]

Call this an exact line search. Setting \(\frac{d}{d\alpha}f(x_k+\alpha p_k)=0\) at \(\alpha=\alpha_k\) gives one identity, and it is worth extracting before the question:

\[ r_{k+1}^{\mathsf T}p_k=0. \]

Read with the ordinary dot product, this says the new residual is perpendicular to the direction just used. Read instead with the inner product \(\langle u,v\rangle_A=u^{\mathsf T}Av\) — legitimate here precisely because \(A\) is positive definite — and using \(r_k=-Ae_k\) for the error \(e_k=x_k-x^\star\), the same identity says

\[ \langle e_{k+1},p_k\rangle_A=0. \]

So whatever else a step does, it removes the error's component along the direction it used, as measured by \(A\). Two more notations to carry: for a symmetric positive definite matrix the eigen-decomposition \(A=Q\Lambda Q^{\mathsf T}\) makes the \(A\)-inner product diagonal, \(\langle q_i,q_j\rangle_A=\lambda_i\delta_{ij}\), so a statement about \(A\)-components is a statement about how the error is distributed over the \(q_i\); and \(\kappa=\lambda_{\max}/\lambda_{\min}\) measures how eccentric the level sets above are.

Nothing in the identities above used anything about the shape of the ellipses. That is the whole toolkit for the first question.

2. Why can the greedy direction not keep the promise?

Question: the line-search identity says that after each step the error is \(A\)-orthogonal to the direction just used. Take two consecutive steps of the greedy method, \(p_k=r_k\) and \(p_{k+1}=r_{k+1}\), and work out what step \(k\)'s guarantee is worth after step \(k+1\). Which single inner product decides whether it survives, and why is that quantity not zero on the running example? Then name the invariant a direction sequence would have to maintain instead.

Figure 2 draws the first three residuals of that greedy run from a common origin, so that they can be compared as directions rather than as arrows at different points. Successive residuals are perpendicular — that is step \(k\)'s identity doing its job. What happens to the guarantee over two steps is the question.

Two facts about the run are worth having in hand. The step length for the greedy choice collapses to \(\alpha_k=r_k^{\mathsf T}r_k/(r_k^{\mathsf T}Ar_k)\), one division, so the direction costs nothing beyond the matrix–vector product \(Ar_k\) that the residual update already needs. And \(A\) here has \(\kappa=2.618\), which is a gentle eccentricity — whatever goes wrong in Figure 1 is not a badly conditioned problem.

3. Where do useful directions come from, without storing everything?

Question: you want a direction family with \(\langle p_i,p_j\rangle_A=0\) for every \(i\neq j\) — called \(A\)-orthogonal, or conjugate with respect to \(A\). Building one by subtraction (take a candidate, remove its \(A\)-projections onto all earlier directions) works, but at step \(k\) it needs every earlier direction and one extra matrix–vector product per correction. Try instead the candidate \(p_k=r_k+\beta_k p_{k-1}\), a single correction against the immediately preceding direction, with \(\beta_k\) fixed by the single condition \(p_k^{\mathsf T}Ap_{k-1}=0\). Show that the \(A\)-inner products of \(r_k\) against \(p_0,\dots,p_{k-2}\) vanish automatically, so no further corrections are needed. Name the two facts doing the work.

Definitions used from here. \(r_k=b-Ax_k\) is the residual and \(p_k\) the search direction. The exact line search fixes a relation between consecutive residuals, obtained by substituting \(\alpha_k\) into the residual update:

\[ r_{k+1}=r_k-\alpha_k Ap_k. \]

Record also, once, what the line searches accumulate. Assume for the moment that the directions are conjugate and every step length is exact; then the residual is orthogonal to every direction used before it,

\[ r_k^{\mathsf T}p_j=0\qquad(j<k), \]

because each earlier direction was already orthogonal to \(r_k\)'s predecessor, and conjugation plus the exact line search kills the last term. The question is what makes such a family reachable from one-term corrections, when the generic construction needs all of history.

4. What is being minimised, and how many steps should it take?

Question: the step lengths are still exact line searches and the directions are now conjugate. State precisely what \(x_k\) minimises, over which set of vectors, and what that produces at the final step. Then predict, without computing: two matrices of the same size have the same condition number \(\kappa=\lambda_{\max}/\lambda_{\min}=1000\), one with a single eigenvalue \(1000\) and the remaining \(n-1\) all equal to \(1\), the other with eigenvalues spread evenly between \(1\) and \(1000\). How many steps does each take, and which feature of a spectrum does the comparison make relevant beyond \(\kappa\)?

The direction recurrence of §3 has a structural consequence. If \(p_0=r_0\), then the directions and the residuals span the same growing family of subspaces, and that family is the Krylov subspace

\[ \mathrm{span}\{p_0,\dots,p_{k-1}\}=\mathrm{span}\{r_0,Ar_0,\dots,A^{k-1}r_0\}=:\mathcal K_k(A,r_0). \]

Take that as given: it follows from \(p_k=r_k+\beta_kp_{k-1}\) and from \(r_{k+1}=r_k-\alpha_kAp_k\), and it says the family is built by repeatedly applying \(A\) to the initial residual. Each step therefore enlarges the affine space \(x_0+\mathcal K_k(A,r_0)\) by one dimension, and the step lengths inside it were chosen one at a time, greedily, by exact line search. The question is what all those greedy choices add up to.

For the prediction question, recall that a symmetric positive definite matrix has an orthonormal eigenbasis, so a spectrum is just a list of positive numbers, and that a step of this method costs one multiplication by \(A\).

5. What does the procedure actually produce on the example?

Question: assemble the procedure from the relations you now have — the step length, the direction update, and the one conjugacy condition that fixes \(\beta_k\) — then run it by hand on the running example from \(x_0=0\). Predict the iterate sequence and the number of steps needed to reach \(x^\star\) exactly. Then, for a sparse \(A\) with a million unknowns, say what one step costs in matrix–vector products, what has to be carried from one step to the next, and what that buys compared with factoring \(A\).

Inputs and outputs. The procedure takes \(A\), \(b\) and \(x_0\), and returns iterates \(x_k\) and residuals \(r_k\). \(A\) enters only through products of the form \(Av\); the entries of \(A\) are never inspected. The relations already established are

\[ x_{k+1}=x_k+\alpha_k p_k,\qquad \alpha_k=\frac{r_k^{\mathsf T}p_k}{p_k^{\mathsf T}Ap_k}, \]
\[ p_k=r_k+\beta_k p_{k-1},\qquad p_k^{\mathsf T}Ap_{k-1}=0. \]

with \(p_0=r_0\). The second line is recursive: \(\beta_k\) is the only freedom left in it, and the conjugacy condition opposite it is one scalar equation, so it determines \(\beta_k\) uniquely — provided the denominator it produces is not zero.

One piece of the procedure is still missing and cannot come from the algebra: it has no way of knowing it is finished other than by looking at the size of \(r_k\). Decide what it should test.

6. What survives when \(A\) stops being positive definite?

Question: keep the same problem, the same quadratic and the same procedure, but make \(A\) symmetric and indefinite — the instance below is one such case. Run the first step of your procedure on it by hand: which quantity does the step ask you to compute, and what is that quantity's value here? Then go back through §1–§4 and list every place positive definiteness was doing work, and what each of those places was doing. Finally: if \(A\) were not symmetric either, but you could still compute products \(x\mapsto Ax\), which of the properties you used would you have to give up?
\[ A=\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{bmatrix},\qquad b=\begin{bmatrix}1\\0\end{bmatrix},\qquad x_0=\begin{bmatrix}0\\0\end{bmatrix}. \]

For a symmetric indefinite \(A\), \(f\) is still smooth and its gradient is still \(Ax-b\), but it is no longer convex. Its stationary point is a saddle, and there are directions along which \(f\) decreases without bound. Figure 3 shows the level sets of this instance; the search direction taken from \(x_0\) is drawn on it.

Everything else in the page applies verbatim to this instance: the gradient is still \(Ax-b\), the residual is still \(b-Ax_k\), the step-length formula is the same fraction, and the conjugacy condition is the same scalar equation. The lines above are the level sets \(x_1(x_2-1)=c\) for evenly spaced \(c\), and the marked point is where \(\nabla f\) vanishes.
