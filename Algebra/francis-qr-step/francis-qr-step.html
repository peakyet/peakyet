Section 1

Why eigenvalues need their own algorithm

Question this section answers

You already know \(\det(A-\lambda I)=0\). Why is that not how anyone actually computes eigenvalues?

The mental model to carry forward

The algorithm never computes an eigenvalue directly. It keeps rotating the basis until the eigenvalues can be read off. Sections 2–5 are about making that rotation cheap, fast, and accurate.

Three routes to the eigenvalues Two of them are taught in every first course. Neither one scales.

  • Expand the determinant. Write out \(p(\lambda)=\det(A-\lambda I)\) and find its roots. For the matrix this deck uses throughout, \(C=\operatorname{circ}(1,2,3,4)\), that polynomial is \(p(\lambda)=\lambda^4-4\lambda^3-44\lambda^2-144\lambda-160\). Correct — but at \(n=100\) it is a degree-100 polynomial, and its roots are a different problem.
  • Power iteration. Multiply a vector by \(A\) over and over. It returns one eigenvalue: the largest in modulus.
  • Change the matrix instead of the polynomial. Apply orthogonal similarities until the eigenvalues are forced onto the diagonal. That is the QR algorithm, and it is what this deck is about.

The failure mode

Route 1 throws away the matrix — the object with the good numerical properties — and hands you a polynomial instead. Route 2 hands you one eigenvalue out of \(n\). For an \(n\times n\) matrix you want all \(n\) of them.

What the algorithm promises And why “backward stable” is the right promise to make.

  • All \(n\) eigenvalues, not one.
  • \(\mathcal{O}(n^3)\) total — the cost of a few matrix factorisations, not of a polynomial root hunt.
  • Real arithmetic, even when the eigenvalues are complex.
  • Backward stable: the numbers it returns are the exact eigenvalues of a matrix within rounding distance of yours.

It delivers them by driving the matrix to real Schur form: block upper triangular, with \(1\times1\) blocks carrying real eigenvalues and \(2\times2\) blocks carrying complex conjugate pairs.

Why that is the strongest honest promise

Take \(A=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}1&1000\\0&1\end{smallmatrix}\right]\). Change the single zero entry \(a_{21}\) to \(10^{-16}\) and the eigenvalues move from \(1\) to \(1\pm 3.162\times10^{-7}\).

A \(10^{-16}\) change in the data moves the answer by \(3.2\times10^{-7}\) — a factor of \(3\times10^{9}\). No algorithm can undo that; the sensitivity belongs to the matrix. What an algorithm can promise is that it adds no error of its own, which is exactly backward stability.

The matrix we will watch Small enough to print, with eigenvalues known in closed form.

\[ C=\operatorname{circ}(1,2,3,4) =\begin{bmatrix}1&2&3&4\\4&1&2&3\\3&4&1&2\\2&3&4&1\end{bmatrix} \]

\(C\) is a circulant, so the discrete Fourier matrix diagonalises it. With \(\omega=e^{2\pi ik/4}\) and \(\lambda_k=\sum_j c_j\omega^{jk}\), the eigenvalues are known in closed form:

\[ \lambda\in\{10,\;-2,\;-2+2i,\;-2-2i\}. \]

Check: \(\operatorname{trace}C=4=\sum_k\lambda_k\).

Predict before the next section

Prediction prompt

The QR step replaces \(A\) by \(A'=RQ\) when \(A=QR\). Do \(A\) and \(A'\) have the same eigenvalues?

Tempting answer: no — \(QR\neq RQ\), so surely the matrix has changed.

Why that fails: \(A'=RQ=Q^{\mathsf T}AQ\) — an orthogonal similarity, the same matrix in a rotated basis. Eigenvalues are exactly what a similarity cannot change, so the step may move every entry as long as it leaves the spectrum alone.

Takeaway

The characteristic polynomial turns an \(n\times n\) matrix into a degree-\(n\) polynomial and hands you a worse-conditioned problem; power iteration returns one eigenvalue out of \(n\). The QR algorithm works on the matrix itself and returns all of them.

Section 2

The QR step, and why it is too slow

Question this section answers

What does one QR step actually do to the matrix — and if it is a similarity, why does running it not finish the job quickly?

The mental model to carry forward

The step multiplies the whole basis by \(A\) and re-orthonormalises it. Each subdiagonal then falls at a fixed ratio of two eigenvalue moduli — so its speed is set by the spectrum, not by the algorithm, and equal moduli stall it outright.

One step: \(A=QR\), then \(A'=RQ\) The whole algorithm is this, repeated — and the eigenvalues never move.

Factor \(A=QR\) with \(Q\) orthogonal and \(R\) upper triangular, then swap the factors:

\[ A=QR \quad\Longrightarrow\quad A'=RQ . \]

The swap is an orthogonal similarity:

\[ A'=RQ=Q^{\mathsf T}(QR)Q=Q^{\mathsf T}AQ . \]

The same matrix in a rotated basis — the spectrum is untouched, which is what lets it rewrite every entry.

Cost per step: a full QR factorisation, \(\mathcal{O}(n^3)\). Section 3 removes that; this section counts steps.

What the step is really doing It is power iteration, run on the entire basis at once.

Iterate \(k\) times and the factors accumulate into the QR factorisation of \(A^k\):

\[ A^k=Q_kR_k . \]
  • The columns of \(Q_k\) are the columns of \(A^k\) re-orthonormalised: column 1 is power iteration on \(\boldsymbol{e}_1\), and \(h_{11}\) becomes its Rayleigh quotient — the eigenvalue.
  • Because that basis is nested, the eigenvalues surface largest modulus first.

The rate is a ratio of eigenvalue moduli

Each subdiagonal has its own fixed rate:

\[ |h_{i+1,i}| \;\longrightarrow\; 0 \quad\text{like}\quad \left|\frac{\lambda_{i+1}}{\lambda_i}\right|^{k} \]

Moduli of \(C\): \(10,\;2.828427,\;2.828427,\;2\). So the two vanishing entries should decay at \(0.282843\) and \(0.707107\):

Predict before the next section

Prediction prompt

\(|h_{32}|\) sits at \(2.0000\) and refuses to move. Is that because it needs more sweeps?

Tempting answer: yes — 15 sweeps is not many; let it run longer and it will get there.

Why that fails: its rate is \(|\lambda_3/\lambda_2|=1.0000000000\). A rate of exactly \(1\) means the entry has no reason to shrink at all — after 200 sweeps \(|h_{32}|\) is still \(2.000000\), while \(|h_{21}|\) has reached \(3.5\times10^{-109}\).

Takeaway

One sweep re-orthonormalises \(A^k\), so eigenvalues surface largest modulus first — but each subdiagonal falls only at a ratio of eigenvalue moduli, and stalls when they are equal. Two problems remain: the \(\mathcal{O}(n^3)\) cost per sweep (section 3) and the rate frozen by the spectrum (section 4).
