QR Iteration

Power iteration finds one eigenvalue and destroys every other direction. One reorthogonalising step fixes that — and costs almost nothing.

1. One eigenvalue is easy. How do we get all of them?

Question: we can already find the largest eigenvalue by iterating a single vector. The matrix below has four. What do we have to do to get the other three out of it?

Start with something we can check by hand. Build a matrix from a diagonal matrix \(\Lambda=\mathrm{diag}(5,3,2,1)\) and four Givens rotations whose cosines and sines are rational, then form the symmetric product

\[ S \;=\; Q\,\Lambda\,Q^{\mathsf T},\qquad Q=G_{01}\!\left(\tfrac35,\tfrac45\right)G_{23}\!\left(\tfrac5{13},\tfrac{12}{13}\right) G_{12}\!\left(\tfrac8{17},\tfrac{15}{17}\right)G_{03}\!\left(\tfrac45,\tfrac35\right). \]

Because \(Q\) is orthogonal the eigenvalues of \(S\) are exactly \(5,3,2,1\) — but \(S\) itself is a dense matrix of fractions, with no visible structure:

\[ S=\begin{bmatrix} 2.703330 & 0.642502 & -1.191146 & 0.136449\\ 0.642502 & 3.078123 & -1.322025 & 0.820740\\ -1.191146 & -1.322025 & 2.490081 & 0.120194\\ 0.136449 & 0.820740 & 0.120194 & 2.728466 \end{bmatrix} \]

Two facts about \(S\) are worth holding on to. Its trace is \(11\), and its determinant is \(30\): consistent with \(5+3+2+1\) and \(5\cdot3\cdot2\cdot1\), but neither tells us which four numbers they are. And the moduli \(5,3,2,1\) are all distinct, so every pair of neighbouring eigenvalues has a gap we can point at:

\[ \frac{|\lambda_2|}{|\lambda_1|}=\frac35=0.600,\qquad \frac{|\lambda_3|}{|\lambda_2|}=\frac23=0.667,\qquad \frac{|\lambda_4|}{|\lambda_3|}=\frac12=0.500. \]

Those three numbers will turn out to be the whole theory. Keep them in view.

Power iteration on \(S\) works and gives us \(5\). It also quietly makes every other direction useless, which is the subject of the next section.

Intuition: a matrix is a set of directions with different stretch factors. Power iteration amplifies the most-stretched direction until the others are lost in the rounding; we want to amplify all of them and keep them apart.
Takeaway: the target is the full spectrum of a matrix whose eigenvalues are known to us and invisible in its entries. The three ratios 0.600, 0.667, 0.500 are the only numbers we have to explain the convergence.

2. Why can we not simply iterate on all four directions at once?

Question: power iteration works on one vector, so run it on four — apply \(S\) to the identity and watch each column. Why does that not give four eigenvalues?

Take the columns of the identity as four starting vectors and apply \(S\) repeatedly, rescaling each column so it does not overflow. Nothing is orthogonalised. Measure the worst absolute cosine between any two columns — \(1.0\) means two columns have become the same direction:

The failure is not rounding. Four vectors amplified by \(S\) are \(S^k\) applied to the identity, and if the eigenvalues had strictly different moduli then

\[ S^k \;=\; Q\,\Lambda^k Q^{-1} \;\longrightarrow\; \text{a rank-one matrix in direction }\boldsymbol q_1, \]

because only the \(\lambda_1^k\) term survives once \(|\lambda_1|^k\) dwarfs all the others. Four columns, one rank: three eigenvalues have been thrown away. Saving them means stopping the collapse — after each application, take the four columns and replace them by an orthonormal basis for the same subspace.

So the requirement is now concrete. We need a step that

  1. advances all \(n\) directions by the action of \(S\), and
  2. re-orthonormalises them before the small ones vanish.

Doing that with a dense QR factorisation costs \(O(n^3)\) per sweep, which at this size is already more work than it took to write \(S\) down. Retaining the orthogonality is the part that must be paid for; the question is whether the payment can be made cheaper.

Takeaway: the obstacle is not the iteration, it is the collapse: without re-orthonormalisation the iteration is rank-one in the limit. Anything that keeps the columns orthogonal has to be worth its cost.

3. What single step advances and re-orthonormalises at once?

Question: we want one operation that both applies \(S\) and hands back an orthonormal basis. Which factorisation gives us that, and what does the matrix look like afterwards?

Write \(V_k\) for an orthonormal basis of \(n\) vectors and apply \(S\), then factor the result:

Simultaneous iteration
\[ S\,V_k \;=\; V_{k+1}\,R_{k+1},\qquad V_{k+1}\text{ orthogonal},\ R_{k+1} \text{ upper triangular}. \]

This is exactly the requirement from the last section: \(V_{k+1}\) is an orthonormal basis for the subspace \(S V_k\) spans, and if \(S V_k\) has full column rank then \(V_{k+1}\) carries the same information without the collapse. No directions are lost.

Now here is the step that makes it cheap. Multiply the relation by \(V_k^{\mathsf T}\) and undo the ordering:

\[ A_{k+1} \;:=\; V_{k+1}^{\mathsf T} S\,V_{k+1} \;=\; R_{k+1}\,V_k^{\mathsf T} S\,V_{k+1} \;=\; R_{k+1}\,Q_{k+1}, \]

where \(Q_{k+1}=V_k^{\mathsf T}V_{k+1}\) is again orthogonal. Read the two ends together: the similarity transform of \(S\) by the new basis is the reversed product of its own factorisation. Write it as one line:

Claim: if \(S=QR\) is a QR factorisation of \(S\), then \(RQ\) is orthogonally similar to \(S\), and \(RQ\) is the result of one step of simultaneous iteration on the basis of \(S\).

Reason: \(RQ = Q^{\mathsf T}(QR)Q = Q^{\mathsf T} S Q\), since \(Q^{\mathsf T}Q=I\). The spectral content is untouched and the basis has been rotated into the one that iteration would have produced anyway.

That single substitution — factor, swap the factors, repeat — is the QR iteration. Before using it we have to deal with a practical problem: one QR step on the dense \(S\) produces a dense result, with mass \(0.763\) still sitting below the first subdiagonal. Applying the step repeatedly would cost \(O(n^3)\) every time and would show no triangular structure to read eigenvalues from.

The fix is to pay an \(O(n^3)\) cost once. Householder reflectors turn \(S\) into an upper Hessenberg matrix — zero below the first subdiagonal — and crucially the QR step preserves that shape: the zero pattern below the subdiagonal is exactly what the rotations never disturb. Write \(H_0=Q_0^{\mathsf T}SQ_0\). One sweep from there gives

One unshifted sweep from H₀
\[ A_1=\begin{bmatrix} 4.011953 & -1.328889 & 0.000000 & 0.000000\\ -1.328889 & 3.051130 & 0.641731 & 0.000000\\ 0.000000 & 0.641731 & 2.250117 & -0.815778\\ 0.000000 & 0.000000 & -0.815778 & 1.686800 \end{bmatrix},\qquad A_1=Q_1^{\mathsf T}H_0Q_1. \]

Everything below the first subdiagonal is zero and stays zero under every later sweep, at a cost of \(O(n^2)\) per sweep rather than \(O(n^3)\). The subdiagonal entries \(1.328889, 0.641731, 0.815778\) are now the whole story — as they shrink toward zero the matrix approaches triangular form, and once \(A\) is triangular its diagonal is the spectrum. So the question becomes how fast those entries decay.

Intuition: the QR step does not chase scalars, it rotates the basis. Each rotation is chosen to re-orthogonalise what the previous rotation stretched, and every rotation carries the matrix closer to triangular form, where the eigenvalues sit on the diagonal in plain sight.
Takeaway: one \(QR\) factorization followed by an \(RQ\) product is a similarity that performs one step of simultaneous iteration, and it rotates \(S\) toward triangular form.

4. Why must the subdiagonal decay, and how fast?

Question: the entries below the diagonal are shrinking. Where does their decay rate come from, and can we predict it before running anything?

Run the unshifted sweep, starting from \(H_0\), and print the three subdiagonal magnitudes \(h_{21},h_{32},h_{43}\) as the sweeps accumulate. What we are looking at is each entry's ratio to its value on the previous sweep:

The ratios are the numbers from Figure 1, and they arrive in the right order without anything being tuned. To see why, recall from §3 that after \(k\) sweeps \(A_k=Q_k^{\mathsf T}SQ_k\) with \(Q_k\) the orthogonal factor of \(S^k\). Since the operators are similar, \(Q_k\) must be carrying the basis in which the action of \(S^k\) is ordered by size. Taking columns of the eigenvector matrix \(X\) and writing \(X^{\mathsf T}\) for its inverse,

\[ S^k \;=\; X\,\Lambda^k X^{-1},\qquad \big(S^k\big)_{ij} \;=\; \sum_{t} x_{it}\,\lambda_t^k\,(X^{-1})_{tj}, \]

so the diagonal term \(\lambda_i^k\) dominates the \((i,i)\) entry once \(k\) is large, while the \((i+1,i)\) entry is dominated by the first term that has not been cancelled — the one carrying \(\lambda_{i+1}^k\). Their quotient is what survives:

\[ |h_{i+1,i}^{(k)}| \;\sim\; \left|\frac{\lambda_{i+1}}{\lambda_i}\right|^{k} \qquad (k\to\infty). \]
Claim: for a matrix with a full set of eigenvectors and \(|\lambda_1|>|\lambda_2|>\cdots>|\lambda_n|\), the unshifted QR iteration satisfies \(|h_{i+1,i}^{(k)}|\sim|\lambda_{i+1}/\lambda_i|^{k}\). The convergence is therefore linear, at a rate set by the eigenvalue ratios.

The ratio is not an accident of one example: it is the ratio of the two terms that survive in the \((i+1,i)\) entry of \(S^k\), which is the same quantity the ratio in Figure 1 measures. Equal moduli are excluded — the cancellation argument above needs a strict ordering, and §6 shows what happens when it is absent.

Linear is not good enough. The ratio \(|\lambda_2/\lambda_1|\) here is \(0.6\), so reaching \(10^{-11}\) takes \(67\) sweeps: that is the measured count in the next section. The rate is fixed by the input; the next question is whether the step can be changed so the rate improves.

Takeaway: convergence is linear with ratio \(|\lambda_{i+1}/\lambda_i|\), which we can now predict, and which is far too slow when two eigenvalues are close in magnitude.

5. What does the procedure cost, and what does it produce here?

Question: can the fixed rate be improved without changing the step, and what does the resulting procedure actually cost per sweep?

The ratio \(|\lambda_{i+1}/\lambda_i|\) is fixed, but the matrix we iterate on is not. Replace \(S\) by \(S-\sigma I\) for a number \(\sigma\) and the eigenvalues become \(\lambda_i-\sigma\); the iterated ratios become

\[ \frac{|\lambda_{i+1}-\sigma|}{|\lambda_i-\sigma|}. \]

If \(\sigma\) is close to \(\lambda_i\), the numerator is small and the denominator is not: the rate collapses toward zero. The matrix is restored afterwards by adding \(\sigma I\) back, which changes nothing about the structure. Choosing \(\sigma\) — the shift — is the entire practical content of the algorithm, and the cheapest useful choice reads it off the matrix itself: take the current bottom-right entry \(\sigma=h_{mm}\).

This is the algorithm. Reduce once, then repeat on a shrinking block:

  1. Reduce. Bring \(S\) to upper Hessenberg form \(H_0=Q_0^{\mathsf T}SQ_0\) with Householder reflectors. One-time cost \(O(n^3)\).
  2. Sweep. With the current block of size \(m\), set \(\sigma=h_{mm}\), factor \(H-\sigma I=QR\) (rotations only, because \(H\) is Hessenberg), and set \(H\leftarrow RQ+\sigma I\).
  3. Chase the entry. Repeat until \(|h_{m,m-1}|\) falls below a threshold; then set it to zero, record \(h_{mm}\) as an eigenvalue, and shrink the block to \(m-1\).
  4. Stop. When the block reaches \(1\times1\) (or a \(2\times2\) block that will not split — see §6) the diagonal holds the spectrum.

Step 2 is where the cost claim lives. Because a Hessenberg matrix already has zeros below the first subdiagonal, a Givens rotation acting on rows \(j,j+1\) touches only two rows of \(O(n)\) entries, and there are \(n-1\) of them. 6. What breaks when a pair of eigenvalues shares a magnitude?

Question: §4 assumed the eigenvalue moduli were strictly ordered. Change that one thing — suppose two of the eigenvalues share a modulus — here a complex conjugate pair — and which quantity stops moving?

Take the companion matrix of \((x-5)(x^2+2x+5)=x^3-3x^2-5x-25\), whose spectrum is \(\{5,\,-1+2i,\,-1-2i\}\). The pair has modulus \(\sqrt5\) and the real eigenvalue has modulus \(5\), so the ordering that §4 needed is absent at the bottom of the block. Run the best real shift available:

Sweep|h32||h21|Trailing 2×2 trace
12.26e+005.92e-01−1.500000
109.71e-012.83e-04−2.000478
409.40e-011.60e-16−2.000000

The real eigenvalue \(5\) is gone — \(|h_{21}|\) reaches machine zero — and the 2×2 trace has converged to \(-2\), which is exactly the trace of the pair. But \(|h_{32}|\) never falls. It oscillates around \(0.9\), and no real shift can do better, for a structural reason: a real \(2\times2\) block with complex eigenvalues is \(\alpha I+\beta J\), and no real orthogonal similarity can make a matrix of that shape triangular. The memory of the imaginary part has nowhere to go. A real shift \(\sigma\) is at distance at least \(|\mathrm{Im}\,\lambda|=2\) from the pair, so the ratio \(|\lambda_{\text{pair}}-\sigma|/|\lambda_{\text{other}}-\sigma|\) never becomes small.

What changes: the subdiagonal \(|h_{32}|\) stops decaying, and no real shift can rescue it. Why: the real eigenvalue is isolated as usual, but what remains is a \(2\times2\) block whose eigenvalues are complex, and a real orthogonal similarity cannot make such a block triangular.

The standard repair is to aim at both members of the pair in one step. Take the two shifts \(\sigma_1,\sigma_2\) from the trailing \(2\times2\) block and use their real quadratic \(p(x)=(x-\sigma_1)(x-\sigma_2)\). Applying \(p\) at once keeps the arithmetic real, and the step is implemented implicitly: instead of forming the degree-four product \(p(H)\), compute only its first column

\[ p(H)\boldsymbol e_1 \;=\; H^2\boldsymbol e_1 - s\,H\boldsymbol e_1 + t\,\boldsymbol e_1, \qquad s=\sigma_1+\sigma_2,\quad t=\sigma_1\sigma_2, \]

which is three numbers, and chase the resulting bulge back to Hessenberg form. Verified on the running example: \(p(H)\boldsymbol e_1\) has its fourth entry equal to \(1.4\times10^{-16}\) — genuinely confined to three coordinates — and the first column of the implicit step's \(Q\) makes an angle of \(8.5\times10^{-7}\) degrees with it. Factorising \(p(H)\) explicitly and comparing the two steps gives \(\|H_{\text{implicit}}-H_{\text{explicit}}\|_{\max}= 1.1\times10^{-15}\), so the implicit route produces the same step for \(O(n^2)\) work instead of \(O(n^3)\). This is the Francis double shift, and the \(s,t\) that specify it are exactly the two numbers a real code stores.

On the complex example the double shift isolates the pair and leaves a \(2\times2\) block whose trace is \(-2.00000000\) and determinant is \(+5.00000000\) — the pair \(-1\pm2i\), read off without any complex arithmetic. The block cannot shrink any further; that residual block is what the real Schur form means. Avoiding complex arithmetic costs that \(2\times 2\) block; using it instead would roughly double storage and quadruple the multiplies in the inner loop, which is why production codes stay real.

Two further limits are worth stating plainly, because both appear in shipped code. Shifts can be pathological: if the trailing \(2\times2\) is nilpotent, the shift it produces is zero, and the sweep makes no progress at all. LAPACK's dlahqr handles this with an explicit exceptional-shift branch before falling back to the Francis double shift. And convergence is guaranteed only under conditions that are genuinely hard to certify — the eigenvalue ratio must not be too close to one and the eigenvectors must not be too nearly parallel. Rapid convergence of the shifted iteration on non-symmetric matrices was only proved in the 2020s, for matrices of bounded eigenvector condition number with an extra exceptional-shift rule, and the guarantees remain more delicate than the symmetric case.

Takeaway: the guarantee in §4 is conditional on a strict modulus ordering. Breaking it does not break the algorithm — it changes its output from a triangular matrix to a quasi-triangular one, leaves a real \(2\times2\) block where a complex pair lives, and requires the double shift to get that far.
