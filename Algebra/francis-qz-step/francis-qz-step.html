Section 1

The pencil problem

Question this section answers

Why is \(Ax=\lambda Bx\) not just an eigenvalue problem for \(B^{-1}A\)?

Two matrices, one equation The pair \((A,B)\) is the object; the inverse is only one way to look at it.

A generalized eigenvalue problem asks for a scalar \(\lambda\) and a nonzero vector \(x\) with

\[ A x = \lambda B x, \qquad (A-\lambda B)x=0, \qquad \det(A-\lambda B)=0. \]

The pair \((A,B)\) is a matrix pencil. If \(B\) is singular, some eigenvalues are infinite, and \(B^{-1}A\) does not exist.

Prediction prompt

If \(B\) is invertible, is forming \(B^{-1}A\) a safe way to compute the eigenvalues?

Tempting: yes — the eigenvalues are the same.

Why it fails: \(B^{-1}A\) can amplify the condition number of \(B\), and it is unavailable when \(B\) is singular.

The failure mode: inversion

Take the smallest example with an infinite eigenvalue:

\[ A=I_2,\qquad B=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\0&0\end{bmatrix}. \]
\[ \det(A-\lambda B)=(1-\lambda)\cdot 1. \]

The pencil has eigenvalues \(\lambda=1\) and \(\lambda=\infty\). But \(B\) is singular, so \(B^{-1}A\) does not exist.

The QZ promise

QZ replaces inversion with two phases of orthogonal transformations:

  • Phase 1. Reduce \((A,B)\) to Hessenberg–triangular form \((H,T)\): \(H\) is upper Hessenberg, \(T\) is upper triangular.
  • Phase 2. Iterate with implicit double-shift steps until \((H,T)\) is quasi-triangular; read the eigenvalues from the diagonal blocks.

Every step is a two-sided orthogonal equivalence \(H\leftarrow Q^{\mathsf T}HZ,\; T\leftarrow Q^{\mathsf T}TZ\). No inverse of \(B\) or of a submatrix of \(B\) is ever formed.

Takeaway

The pair \((A,B)\) is the right object; QZ works on it directly and handles singular \(B\) and infinite eigenvalues without a special case.

Section 2

Hessenberg–triangular form

Question this section answers

What shape should the pencil have before the iteration begins?

Reduce the pair, preserve the eigenvalues Phase 1 is a direct, one-time reduction.

Find orthogonal \(Q,Z\) with

\[ Q^{\mathsf T} A Z = H \quad\text{upper Hessenberg}, \]
\[ Q^{\mathsf T} B Z = T \quad\text{upper triangular}. \]

Eigenvalues are unchanged because

\[ \det(\beta H-\alpha T)=c\,\det(\beta A-\alpha B), \]

with \(c=\det(Q^{\mathsf T})\det(Z)\neq0\).

Why this is the right shape

  • Cheap iteration. A QZ step on \((H,T)\) touches a narrow band and costs \(O(n^2)\), not \(O(n^3)\).
  • A bulge can move. Hessenberg \(H\) and triangular \(T\) are exactly the structures a left–right rotation pair can restore one step at a time.
  • The step is determined by its shifts. The implicit \(Q\) theorem says the whole sweep is essentially unique once the first rotation is chosen — so the shifts control everything.
  • It is the pencil analogue of Hessenberg reduction. The QR algorithm reduces one matrix to Hessenberg form first; QZ reduces the pair to Hessenberg–triangular form first.

The reduction is a sequence of Householder reflectors and Givens rotations. Its construction is the companion note on Householder transformations.

The running example

We will use a \(4\times 4\) pencil already in HT form:

\[ H_0=\begin{bmatrix} 8.5 & 3.7 & 0.8 & 0.4\\ 1.5 & 4.8 & 1.9 & 0.7\\ 0 & 1.0 & 1.6 & 1.8\\ 0 & 0 & -0.5 & 1.0 \end{bmatrix} \]
\[ T_0=\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0.5 & 0.2 & 0.1\\ 0 & 1.5 & 0.3 & 0.2\\ 0 & 0 & 1 & 0.4\\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 0.5 \end{bmatrix} \]

The trailing \(2\times2\) pencil has shifts \(2\pm i\). The four generalized eigenvalues are approximately \(1.790987787\pm0.6940270197i\), \(2.700416827\), and \(4.7176076\).

Takeaway

HT form is the starting line: the eigenvalue problem is unchanged, and the iteration now has a narrow band to chase.

Section 3

The Francis QZ step

Question this section answers

How do you take a double-shift QR step on \(HT^{-1}\) without forming \(HT^{-1}\) or inverting \(T\)?

The QR step, in one breath Everything starts from a three-entry vector.

For a single upper Hessenberg matrix \(A\), an implicit double-shift QR step chooses shifts \(\mu_1,\mu_2\) and forms the first column of

\[ p(A)=(A-\mu_1 I)(A-\mu_2 I). \]

Because \(A\) is Hessenberg, \(p(A)e_1\) has only three nonzeros. That little vector starts the bulge.

The pencil version

For the pencil \((H,T)\), the mental object is \(M=HT^{-1}\). A double shift applies \(p(M)=(M-\mu_1 I)(M-\mu_2 I)\).

\[ p(M)e_1=(H-\mu_1T)\,T^{-1}(H-\mu_2T)\,T^{-1}e_1. \]

Compute it with two triangular solves and two matrix–vector products:

\[ Tw=e_1,\qquad u=(H-\mu_2T)w,\qquad Ty=u,\qquad v=(H-\mu_1T)y. \]

The vector \(v\) is the bulge vector. It determines the first Householder reflection. \(T\) is never inverted.

Why a double shift?

A real pencil can have complex eigenvalues, and they arrive in conjugate pairs. A single complex shift would force complex arithmetic through the whole sweep.

\[ (M-\mu I)(M-\bar\mu I)=M^2-2\operatorname{Re}(\mu)M+|\mu|^2I. \]

The product is real even when \(\mu\) is not. That is why Francis's double shift is the natural step for real matrices: one real sweep handles a conjugate pair.

The shifts come from the trailing \(2\times2\) pencil. If its eigenvalues are real, the same double shift is simply two real shifts.

Takeaway

A double shift keeps a real pencil real while chasing a complex-conjugate pair.

Section 4

Chasing the bulge

Question this section answers

How does a small dense bulge move down the pencil while both structures are restored?

The bulge has two sides

The initial left Householder knocks \(H\) out of Hessenberg form and \(T\) out of triangular form.

A right rotation restores \(T\) but creates a bulge in \(H\); a left rotation restores \(H\) but creates a bulge in \(T\). They must alternate.

Prediction prompt

Can you restore both structures with rotations on only one side?

Tempting: yes — apply the same rotation to both.

Why it fails: the transformation is \(Q^{\mathsf T}HZ\), not a similarity. Left rotations belong to \(H\); right rotations belong to \(T\).

The chase, drawn

  • A left \(3\times3\) Householder zeros the bulge in \(H\).
  • A right \(3\times3\) Householder zeros the bulge in \(T\).
  • At the bottom, two Givens rotations finish the sweep.

What one step preserves

  • \(H\) remains upper Hessenberg.
  • \(T\) remains upper triangular.
  • \(Q\) and \(Z\) are orthogonal.
  • The pencil is unchanged up to orthogonal equivalence.
\[ H_1=Q^{\mathsf T}H_0Z,\qquad T_1=Q^{\mathsf T}T_0Z. \]

Takeaway

One QZ step is a double-shift QR step on \(HT^{-1}\), realized as a two-sided orthogonal equivalence that preserves the Hessenberg–triangular structure.

Section 5

Shifts, deflation, convergence

Question this section answers

What turns a correct QZ step into an algorithm that actually finishes?

Where the shifts come from

The shifts are the generalized eigenvalues of the trailing \(2\times2\) pencil:

\[ \det(H_{22}-\mu T_{22})=0. \]

In the real case, take the pair whose roots are closest to the bottom-right ratio \(H_{nn}/T_{nn}\) — the Wilkinson shift. If the pair is complex, use it as a double shift.

An exceptional shift (a fixed real shift every ten iterations, say) breaks cycles when the ordinary shifts stall.

Deflation: when to stop chasing

A subdiagonal entry is negligible when

\[ |H_{i+1,i}|\le \varepsilon\,(|H_{i,i}|+|H_{i+1,i+1}|). \]

Then the pencil splits into two independent subproblems. A negligible diagonal entry of \(T\) means an infinite eigenvalue that can be deflated, and later steps only touch the remaining window.

Takeaway

Shifts steer the step; deflation decides when a piece is finished.

Cost and convergence

One QZ step costs \(O(n^2)\); the whole iteration costs \(O(n^3)\), the same order as the initial reduction. For well-separated eigenvalues, convergence is locally quadratic.

Limits and modern variants

  • Dense, direct, small-to-medium. QZ computes the whole spectrum of a dense pencil.
  • Not for very large sparse pencils. There, use Arnoldi, Lanczos, or rational Krylov methods that target part of the spectrum.
  • Multishift + aggressive early deflation. Chase several bulges at once and deflate converged eigenvalues early.
  • Rational QZ and pole swapping. Generalize the step beyond Hessenberg–triangular form and allow rational filters.

The trade

Classical QZ is simple and backward stable. Modern variants keep the same step but change the schedule: more shifts, earlier deflation, better memory behaviour.

The one-sentence version

Takeaway

A Francis QZ step is a double-shift QR step on \(HT^{-1}\), computed implicitly: two triangular solves produce a three-entry bulge vector, and alternating left/right orthogonal rotations chase that bulge down the pencil while preserving its Hessenberg–triangular structure.

If you remember one picture, remember the two-sided bulge: left rotation for \(H\), right rotation for \(T\), one step down at a time.
