Householder Transformations: Zeroing Out with a Mirror

You hand your solver a least-squares problem and it refuses the textbook method. Not Gram–Schmidt, not row reduction — instead, every serious solver (LAPACK, NumPy, MATLAB, Julia) reflects the coordinate system in a sequence of mirrors, each described by two short arrays and never assembled into a matrix. That idea is Householder's 1958 trick. On this page you will build the mirror from nothing but a perpendicular bisector, prove in one line that it is free to apply, discover that a single sign choice separates machine-zero from eight digits of garbage, and see why this same mirror is what makes eigenvalue solvers work. Three debts we open now and collect later: why the "matrix" never exists (§3–5), why the sign of one term decides everything (§4), and why Gaussian elimination is banned from eigenvalue software but mirrors are not (§7).

1. Why solvers refuse Gram–Schmidt

Here is the job. You have \(A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}\) with \(m\ge n\) and want the least-squares fit \(\min_x\|Ax-b\|\). The stable route is to rotate the coordinate system until \(A\) becomes upper triangular: find an orthogonal \(Q\) (meaning \(Q^{-1}=Q^{\mathsf T}\)) with

\[ Q^{\mathsf T}A \;=\; R \qquad\text{(upper triangular)},\qquad\; Q^{\mathsf T}b \;=\; c \;=\;\begin{bmatrix}c_1\\ c_2\end{bmatrix},\qquad\; x^{\ast}=R^{-1}c_1 \]

Why insist on orthogonal? Because orthogonality is the property that leaves the objective itself untouched: rotations and reflections preserve \(\|\cdot\|_2\), so the problem you solve after the transformation is the same problem in new coordinates. Skew transformations — Gaussian elimination's weapon — distort the residual; that's the seed of the \(A^{\mathsf T}A\) normal-equations trap, which silently squares the condition number.

So compute the orthogonal \(Q\) with the textbook Gram–Schmidt process and you're done, right? No — and this is the first failure you should actually see. Run classical Gram–Schmidt on a Hilbert matrix (harmless-looking entries, \(\kappa(H_{12})\approx10^{16}\)) and the returned "\(\hat Q\)" is not orthogonal at all: \(\|\hat Q^{\mathsf T}\hat Q - I\|_F = \mathcal{O}(1)\). Its columns drift away from perpendicular by more than they keep to it.

Figure 1. Orthogonality left behind. As the Hilbert matrix's condition number climbs, classical Gram–Schmidt's computed \(Q\) (red) drifts to \(\mathcal{O}(1)\) deviation; modified Gram–Schmidt (gold) holds longer but follows; Householder QR (blue) sits at \(\sim10^{-15}\) for every \(\kappa\) tried. Both GS variants still give a small \(\|QR-A\|\) — the failure is precisely in the orthogonality promise you were counting on. (All numbers from householder_demo.py and a Wolfram cross-check, both in this folder.)

Now the twist that decides which tool wins: there is a second consumer of these zeroing-out transformations — eigenvalue solvers — and it has an even stricter demand. For a symmetric \(A=A^{\mathsf T}\), the transformation must be a similarity \(A\mapsto P^{-1}AP\) that keeps symmetry, because all the magic of real eigenvalues and orthogonal eigenvectors lives in that structure. \(P^{-1}AP\) stays symmetric exactly when \(P^{-1}=P^{\mathsf T}\) and \(P=P^{\mathsf T}\)... which is a peculiar request: a matrix that is its own inverse and its own transpose at the same time. Gaussian elimination cannot produce it. A mirror can — collect that in §7.

So the shopping list for the tool that runs numerical linear algebra is: (i) orthogonal — never amplifies error, preserves what you measure; (ii) kills up to \(n-1\) entries of a vector in one move; (iii) costs \(\mathcal{O}(n)\) to apply, with an inverse you never have to compute. Householder's answer is one formula and one integer-sized decision.

Takeaway: QR for least squares and tridiagonalization for eigenvalues both need orthogonal eliminations — and Gram–Schmidt breaks exactly that promise, which is why we must build a better zero-maker.

2. The mirror trick: what a reflection is, and how to aim it

Forget matrices for a moment and think geometrically. We want to send a nonzero \(\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^n\) onto the \(e_1\)-axis by a transformation that preserves all lengths — kill the tail \((x_2,\dots,x_n)^{\mathsf T}\) stone dead while touching nothing else we care about. If lengths are preserved, the landing point is forced: a vector parallel to \(e_1\) with length \(\mu\coloneqq\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_2\) can only be \(\alpha e_1\) with \(\alpha=\pm\mu\). Two possible targets — and (spoiler from Figure 1 territory) we get to choose, which is the sign decision the whole art rests on. §4 settles it; here, take any \(\alpha\) with \(|\alpha|=\mu\).

What transformation swaps \(\boldsymbol{x}\leftrightarrow\alpha e_1\) while preserving lengths and being its own inverse? A reflection. A Householder reflection flips space through a hyperplane (an \((n{-}1)\)-dimensional "mirror" through the origin): everything in the mirror is fixed, everything perpendicular to it is negated. Where should the mirror sit? A reflection is an isometry that swaps \(\boldsymbol{x}\) and \(\alpha e_1\), so every point of the mirror must be equidistant from the pair: the mirror is the perpendicular bisector of the chord \(\boldsymbol{x}-\alpha e_1\). And here the Pythagoras of it pays off — because \(\|\boldsymbol{x}\|=\|\alpha e_1\|=\mu\), the triangle \(\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{x},\alpha e_1\) is isosceles, so that bisector passes through the origin automatically (a reflection through an offset plane wouldn't be linear). One-line check: the chord is perpendicular to the midpoint,

\[ (\boldsymbol{x}-\alpha e_1)^{\mathsf T}(\boldsymbol{x}+\alpha e_1) \;=\; \|\boldsymbol{x}\|^2-\alpha^2 \;=\; \mu^2-\mu^2 \;=\; 0 .\]

So the mirror's normal vector is exactly the chord:

\[ \boldsymbol{v} \;=\; \boldsymbol{x}-\alpha e_1 \qquad(\text{pick }\alpha=\pm\mu), \]

and both sign choices produce a geometrically perfect mirror — Figure 2. That's the punchline of the construction: the mirror isn't clever linear algebra, it's the perpendicular bisector, and there are only ever two of them.

Figure 2. The two — and only two — mirrors that send \(\boldsymbol{x}=(4,3)^{\mathsf T}\) onto the \(e_1\)-axis. Each mirror is the perpendicular bisector of the chord from \(\boldsymbol{x}\) to its target; the chord is the mirror's normal. Both reflectors are mathematically perfect. Only one survives floating-point arithmetic (§4).

Read the picture once more, because it earns its keep in §4: the naive fold sends \(\boldsymbol{x}\) to the target on its own side (short chord — the normal nearly cancels when \(\boldsymbol{x}\) already points along \(e_1\)), while the safe fold sends \(\boldsymbol{x}\) past the origin to the opposite axis (long chord — components add, never subtract). Same task, opposite robustness.

Intuition: you never choose the reflection's effect on the rest of space — the mirror decides. What you choose is only the target (\(\pm\mu e_1\)), and the target fixes the normal \(\boldsymbol{v}=\boldsymbol{x}-\alpha e_1\). Every "trick" in the rest of this article is really just this one line, read differently.
Takeaway: to zero a vector's tail by a length-preserving map, reflect through the perpendicular bisector of the chord to \(\pm\mu e_1\); isosceles geometry guarantees the mirror passes through the origin.

3. Rank-one algebra: the mirror you can compute and the matrix that never exists

Turn the construction into arithmetic. For any nonzero \(\boldsymbol{v}\) (we'll set it to the chord from §2), split \(\boldsymbol{x}\) into the part along \(\boldsymbol{v}\) and the part in the mirror \(\boldsymbol{v}^{\perp}\), then negate only the first part:

\[ H\boldsymbol{x} \;=\; \boldsymbol{x}-2\,\mathrm{proj}_{\boldsymbol{v}}\boldsymbol{x} \;=\; \boldsymbol{x}-2\,\frac{\boldsymbol{v}^{\mathsf T}\boldsymbol{x}}{\boldsymbol{v}^{\mathsf T}\boldsymbol{v}}\,\boldsymbol{v} \;=\; \Big(I-\beta\,\boldsymbol{v}\boldsymbol{v}^{\mathsf T}\Big)\boldsymbol{x}, \qquad \beta \coloneqq \frac{2}{\boldsymbol{v}^{\mathsf T}\boldsymbol{v}} .\]
\[ \boxed{\;H \;=\; I-\beta\,\boldsymbol{v}\boldsymbol{v}^{\mathsf T}\;} \qquad\text{with}\qquad \boldsymbol{v}=\boldsymbol{x}-\alpha e_1,\;\; \alpha=\pm\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_2 \;\;\Longrightarrow\;\; H\boldsymbol{x}=\alpha e_1 .\]

That the boxed choice works is a two-line expansion — and it works for either sign, which we verified symbolically in all its gory generality (Wolfram, exact arithmetic): with \(\mu^2=\alpha^2\),

\[ \boldsymbol{v}^{\mathsf T}\boldsymbol{x} = \mu^2-\alpha x_1 = \alpha(\alpha-x_1), \qquad \boldsymbol{v}^{\mathsf T}\boldsymbol{v} = \mu^2-2\alpha x_1+\alpha^2 = 2\alpha(\alpha-x_1),\]
\[ \gamma \coloneqq \beta\,\boldsymbol{v}^{\mathsf T}\boldsymbol{x} = \frac{2\,\alpha(\alpha-x_1)}{2\alpha(\alpha-x_1)} = 1 \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad H\boldsymbol{x} = \boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{v} = \alpha e_1 .\]

Note the almost suspicious-looking \(\gamma=1\): reflecting a vector across its own chord-bisector costs you nothing but subtracting the chord. Keep that identity — §5 plays it on our running example, and it explains why the arithmetic below stays in clean fractions.

Three one-line properties are what make the mirror the workhorse of numerical linear algebra. Write them down once and they pay rent all article (each verified exactly in a computer-algebra kernel):

4. The sign bit: a cancellation disaster you can delete from your code

The algebra of §3 is sign-blind: both mirrors are perfect. So if you were writing the kernel yourself, you'd probably pick \(\alpha=+\mu\) and take the short path — fold \(\boldsymbol{x}\) onto the axis in front of it. LAPACK does the opposite. Someone made the "weirder" choice: the long way past the origin, \(\alpha=-\operatorname{sign}(x_1)\,\mu\). Why would production software choose the longer fold?

Predict: with \(\boldsymbol{x}=(1,\,10^{-8},\,0,\,0)\) and IEEE double arithmetic (\(u=1.1\times10^{-16}\)), which choice fails, and roughly how badly? (a) the safe long fold fails sometimes, (b) the short fold leaves the \(10^{-8}\) entry fully unzeroed, (c) both give machine-zero.

(b). Trace it by hand: \(\hat\mu=\mathrm{fl}\sqrt{1+10^{-16}} = 1\) exactly (\(10^{-16}\) is below half an ulp of 1), so the short-fold normal becomes \(\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}_1 = (1-\hat\mu,\,10^{-8}) = (0,\,10^{-8})\) — a vector pointing along \(e_2\), not along the intended \((-5\times10^{-17},10^{-8})\). You built a mirror, but the wrong one: the mirror perpendicular to \(e_2\) is the \(e_1\)-axis, and it maps \(\boldsymbol{x}\mapsto(1,-10^{-8})\). Nothing got zeroed — the reflector just flipped the sign of the entry you were trying to kill. The long fold gives \(\boldsymbol{v}_2=(2+5\!\times\!10^{-17},10^{-8})\), built entirely from same-sign sums, and \(H\boldsymbol{x}=(-1,\sim10^{-17}\text{ leftovers})\): zero to the last bit. Run demo3 in householder_demo.py to see exactly this table.

What went wrong is the oldest villain in floating point: catastrophic cancellation. The computed norm \(\hat\mu=\mu(1+\delta)\), \(|\delta|\le u\), carries an absolute error of order \(u\mu\). The safe normal's first component is the sum \(x_1+\mu\) (magnitude \(\ge\mu\)), so \(u\mu\) is a relative error of size \(u\) — harmless. The naive normal's first component is the difference \(x_1-\mu = -\tfrac{1}{2}\|\text{tail}\|^2/x_1\) — its own size is \(\|\text{tail}\|^2/2\mu\). The absolute error \(u\mu\) divided by the true component is relative garbage of size \(u/\varepsilon^2\) when \(\|\text{tail}\|=\varepsilon\mu\); in direction terms, \(\|\text{direction error of }\hat{\boldsymbol v}\|\approx 2u/\varepsilon\) — and the leftover after reflection is twice that, scaled by \(\mu\):

\[ \text{leftover}\;\approx\; \frac{2u\,\mu^2}{\|\text{tail}\|} \;\;\text{until}\;\; \|\text{tail}\|\lesssim \sqrt{2u}\,\mu\approx 1.5\!\times\!10^{-8}\mu,\quad\text{then it saturates at}\quad\text{leftover}\approx\|\text{tail}\| .\]

The crossover \(\sqrt{u}\) — half the mantissa's bits flushed down the drain by one subtraction — is visible as the knee in Figure 3.

Figure 3. \(\boldsymbol{x}=(1,\varepsilon)^{\mathsf T}\), IEEE double. With the naive short fold (red), the leftover entry grows as \(2u/\varepsilon\) — the mirror's angle carries relative error \(u/\varepsilon\) — until \(\varepsilon<\sqrt{2u}\) where it degenerates into an axis flip and the "zero" stays as big as \(\varepsilon\) itself. The safe long fold (blue) lands at or below \(u\) for every \(\varepsilon\) tried. Computed with demo3; plot exported from Wolfram.

Two things to hold onto. First, the fix is free: \(\alpha=-\operatorname{sign}(x_1)\mu\) costs one branch and buys the whole first quadrant of numerical analysis — the safe normal also satisfies \(|v_1| = |x_1|+\mu \ge \mu \ge \max_i|x_i|\), so it is always the largest component and always safe to divide by, which is exactly how LAPACK stores reflectors (§5). (If the tail is exactly zero, the naive fold degenerates to \(\boldsymbol{v}=\boldsymbol{0}\) — division by zero — and dlarfg simply returns \(H=I\): no zeros to introduce, no mirror needed.) Second, notice how the naive version fails: it still produced an exactly orthogonal matrix (you constructed it as a reflection, after all) pointing in the wrong direction. Householder transforms cannot produce error-amplification even when the parameters are garbage — at worst they give you a slightly mis-aimed perfect mirror. That is a structural guarantee Gram–Schmidt's column arithmetic simply does not have, and it is the seed of the backward-stability theorem we collect in §6.

A published wrinkle: the sign choice is not merely folklore tidiness. In weighted least squares with row norms spanning 12 orders of magnitude, Cohen & Higham (SIAM J. Matrix Anal. Appl. 19, 1998) proved Householder QR is row-wise backward stable for exactly one of the two sign choices — the safe one. When the stakes are exotic, the naive mirror really does lose digits.
Takeaway: always fold the target away from \(x_1\), \(\alpha=-\operatorname{sign}(x_1)\mu\): mathematically either sign works, but only the long chord survives IEEE arithmetic, and it doubles as the stable pivot for storage.

\[ H \;=\; I-\tfrac{1}{45}\boldsymbol{v}\boldsymbol{v}^{\mathsf T} \;=\; \begin{pmatrix} -\tfrac45 & -\tfrac15 & -\tfrac25 & -\tfrac25\\[2pt] -\tfrac15 & \tfrac{44}{45} & -\tfrac{2}{45} & -\tfrac{2}{45}\\[2pt] -\tfrac25 & -\tfrac{2}{45} & \tfrac{41}{45} & -\tfrac{4}{45}\\[2pt] -\tfrac25 & -\tfrac{2}{45} & -\tfrac{4}{45} & \tfrac{41}{45}\end{pmatrix}, \qquad H^{\mathsf T}=H,\; H^2=I,\; \det H=-1 \;\checkmark \]

as verified symbolically (a computer-algebra kernel confirms \(H^{\mathsf T}H=I\) and eigenvalues \(\{-1,1,1,1\}\) exactly). What actually lives in memory, LAPACK-style (§4's storage bonus), is the pivot-normalized chord and one scalar:

\[ \hat{\boldsymbol{v}} = \boldsymbol{v}/v_1 = \big(1,\tfrac19,\tfrac29,\tfrac29\big),\qquad \tau = \frac{\alpha-x_1}{\alpha} = \tfrac95,\qquad H = I-\tau\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}^{\mathsf T}, \]

That is the whole algorithm's footprint per column: \(n\) reals plus one scalar, applied in \(4n\) flops.

Takeaway: for \(\boldsymbol{x}=(4,1,2,2)^{\mathsf T}\): \(\boldsymbol{v}=(9,1,2,2)\), \(\beta=\tfrac1{45}\), \(\gamma=1\), one subtraction delivers \((-5,0,0,0)\) — and the identity \(\boldsymbol{v}^{\mathsf T}\boldsymbol{x}=\tfrac12\boldsymbol{v}^{\mathsf T}\boldsymbol{v}\) makes \(\gamma\equiv 1\) for every freshly-built reflector.

6. Householder QR: zeros that stay zero

A single mirror kills one vector's tail. To triangularize a whole matrix, aim the mirror at one column at a time — with one subtlety that makes the loop terminate. After the first reflector has zeroed column 1, the next reflector must not touch rows above its target. The solution is trivial by construction: use \(H_2=\mathrm{diag}(1,\hat H_2)\), a mirror that reflects only rows \(2..m\) and is the identity above. A transformation that is the identity on rows where your zeros live cannot move them. Zeros stay zero.

Sanity-check the bookkeeping the way the algorithm does: column 2 of \(R\) holds \((-1.6)^2+(-1.85472)^2 = 2.56+3.44 = 6.0 = \|a_2\|^2\) — the Pythagorean split of a column norm that the orthogonal loop performs at every step. Nothing is ever "combined"; it is only re-pointed.

Predict: after the factorization you solve \(R\hat x = c_1\) by back-substitution (\(c=Q^{\mathsf T}b\), split \(c=(c_1,c_2)\) by rows). Where is the optimal residual \(\min_x\|Ax-b\|\)? (a) nowhere — you must recompute \(A\hat x-b\); (b) hidden in \(c_2\); (c) hidden in \(R\)'s last row.

(b), exactly: \(\min_x\|Ax-b\| = \|c_2\|\) — a by-product, no extra pass, no matrix–vector products. Proof is one line, and it's all orthogonality:

\[ \|A\boldsymbol{x}-b\|^2 = \|Q^{\mathsf T}(A\boldsymbol{x}-b)\|^2 = \left\|\begin{bmatrix}R\\0\end{bmatrix}x-\begin{bmatrix}c_1\\c_2\end{bmatrix}\right\|^2 = \|Rx-c_1\|^2+\|c_2\|^2 ,\]

so choosing \(x\) to zero the first term leaves the second untouched — and the second term doesn't even depend on \(x\). The normal equations throw this information away (they square \(\kappa\) and lose \(\|c_2\|\)). If your application is fit-quality monitoring, Householder QR hands it to you for the cost of indexing.

Cost and reputation. Per reflector the trailing-block update is \(4(m-k+1)(n-k+1)\) flops; the sum is the canonical \(2mn^2-\tfrac23n^3\) (\(\approx\tfrac43n^3\) when \(m=n\)) — about 2× LU's \(2n^3/3\), the same order as classical Gram–Schmidt, but bought with a guarantee: the computed \(\hat Q,\hat R\) satisfy \(\hat Q\hat R = A+\Delta A\) with \(\|\Delta A\|_2 \le p(m,n)\,u\,\|A\|_2\), and \(\hat Q^{\mathsf T}\hat Q = I + p'(m,n)\,u\) — backward stable, with orthogonality to machine precision for any \(\kappa(A)\) (Trefethen & Bau, Lecture 16). That's Figure 1's blue line made theorem — and the §4 "wrong-but-still-orthogonal" phenomenon is its mechanism.

Production detail: LAPACK's dgeqrf is exactly the pseudocode above (with dlarfg = the safe-sign vector builder); dormqr applies the stored reflectors to other matrices; dorgqr assembles \(Q\) only if someone truly needs the matrix. Householder's 1958 paper pitched the method as a cost cut over Givens — \(n\) square roots instead of \(n(n-1)/2\), "highly stable numerically" inherited from Givens' track record; the formal stability proofs arrived with Björck in the 1960s, and Trefethen–Bau Lecture 16 is the modern clean account.

Takeaway: Householder QR is \(\tfrac43n^3\)-ish flops for any \(m\times n\) with a \(\kappa\)-free orthogonality guarantee, never forms \(Q\), and hands you the optimal residual \(\|c_2\|\) for free.

7. Eigenvalues: the same mirror, doubled

§1's third debt, collected. Suppose \(A\) is symmetric and you want its eigenvalues. The classical engine (the QR algorithm) iterates \(Q\)'s-and-\(R\)'s on a structurally reduced matrix, because full QR sweeps on a dense matrix are \(\mathcal{O}(n^3)\) each and would never converge in time. So: zero out a whole chunk and keep the eigenvalues and keep the symmetry. You need a similarity \(T=Q^{\mathsf T}AQ\).

Similarities by orthogonal \(Q\) preserve eigenvalues for free (\(Q^{\mathsf T}AQ\) and \(A\) share the characteristic polynomial). And now the trio from §3 cashes in: take the embedded Householder mirror as \(Q\). Since \(H=H^{\mathsf T}\) and \(H^{-1}=H\), the similarity \(T=HAH\) is symmetric automatically: \(T^{\mathsf T}=H^{\mathsf T}A^{\mathsf T}H^{\mathsf T}=HAH=T\). Compare Gaussian elimination's \(P^{-1}AP\): \(P^{-1}\ne P^{\mathsf T}\), symmetry annihilated, real spectrum lost to complex land, algorithmic chaos ensues. This — not speed — is why GE is banned from eigenvalue software.

The procedure, tridiagonalization, is §6's loop aimed at one column at a time but with the reflector applied on both sides (the second \(H\) is the free inverse — applying the same mirror twice is "undo"):

  1. Take the tail \(y=A(k{+}1{:}m,\,k)\) — for our example \(k=1\): \(y=(1,2,2)^{\mathsf T}\), and note the running example pays again: \(\|y\|=3\), so one mirror sends \((1,2,2)\mapsto(-3,0,0)\), zeroing both of its tail entries in one move.
  2. Form \(H_k=\mathrm{diag}(I_k,\hat H_k)\) from that \(y\) (safe sign) and replace \(A\leftarrow H_kAH_k\). Left multiplication zeros the column below the sub-diagonal; the same \(H_k\) on the right zeroes the corresponding row — and because only half the matrix is independent, the two-sided update is implemented as a symmetric rank-2 patch, which is what keeps the total at \(\tfrac43n^3\) rather than twice that (Golub & Van Loan p.459).
  3. After \(n-2\) mirrors, \(T=Q^{\mathsf T}AQ\) is tridiagonal: same eigenvalues, \(3n\) entries instead of \(n^2\), and the shifted QR algorithm now costs \(\mathcal{O}(n^2)\) per sweep on it — all \(n\) eigenpairs in a handful of sweeps. This is what dsyev does.

Non-symmetric \(A\)? Same loop, one fewer zero per column (a mirror can't kill the entry it reflects into), gives the \(\mathcal{O}(n^3)\) Hessenberg reduction — \(10n^3/3\) flops for QR eigenvalue algorithms' starting point. And the SVD (bidiagonalization) is the mirror applied to two rectangular blocks at once. If your solver's name ends in -ev or -svd, Householder is its preprocessing backbone.

Takeaway: \(H=H^{\mathsf T}=H^{-1}\) makes the similarity \(HAH\) symmetric and eigenvalue-preserving for free, so \(n-2\) mirrors turn any symmetric eigenproblem into a tridiagonal one in \(\tfrac43n^3\) flops — the front door of every real symmetric eigensolver.

8. The wider family: rotations, blocks, and groups

Householder is not the only zero-maker; it competes with its cousin, the Givens rotation, and the boundary between them is pure mechanism:

Figure 6. One column of a matrix, two philosophies: a Givens rotation acts in a chosen 2D coordinate plane (green arc) and removes a single entry per shot; a Householder mirror cuts the whole tail at once. Same Pythagorean bookkeeping, different blast radius.

Three deeper connections, one paragraph each. Mirrors compose into rotations. The product of two Householder reflectors \(H_{\boldsymbol{v}}H_{\boldsymbol{w}}\) is a rotation by twice the angle between the mirrors, living entirely in \(\mathrm{span}\{\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{w}\}\) (det \(+1\)). Conversely the QR factorization of an orthogonal matrix proves every \(Q\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}\) is a product of at most \(n\) Householder reflectors — mirrors generate the whole orthogonal group the way transpositions generate the permutation group. Your 3D rotation matrix, if you ever need to build one without trig: two mirrors.

Blocks make mirrors fast again. The rank-1 recipe is optimal per reflector but starves modern machines of matrix–matrix work. Bischof & Van Loan (SIAM J. Sci. Stat. Comput. 8(1), 1987) noticed that a product of \(b\) successive reflectors collapses into a rank-\(b\) update, the WY form \(H_1\cdots H_b = I - V T V^{\mathsf T}\) with \(V\) unit-lower-trapezoidal and \(T\in\mathbb{R}^{b\times b}\) upper triangular. Apply \(b\) reflectors at once as two matrix products (BLAS-3 gemm): this is LAPACK's blocked dgeqrf, the reason QR runs at a respectable fraction of peak FLOPS instead of stalling at the memory wall — the same "never form the matrix" idea from §3, now forming a small dense one on purpose. (Schreiber & Van Loan's storage-efficient variant, 1989, is what the blocked codes keep in memory.)

The mirror is the atom of the Clifford group. In geometric algebra, reflection of a vector in the hyperplane normal to unit \(\boldsymbol{v}\) is the sandwich \(x\mapsto -\boldsymbol{v}\,x\,\boldsymbol{v}^{-1}\) — the Householder formula with the projection hidden in the Clifford product (see the sibling note on wedge products & geometric algebra). Rotors are even products of reflections (two = rotation, the odd/det\(-1\) class includes the quantum Hadamard gate, \(H_{\text{frac}} = I-2uu^{\mathsf T}\), \(u=(\cos\tfrac\pi8,\sin\tfrac\pi8)\), literally a Householder reflector). Once you see \(I-2\beta\boldsymbol{v}\boldsymbol{v}^{\mathsf T}\) as the way to reflect, you start finding it in places you weren't promised: randomized range finders, rank-revealing QR with column pivoting, Arnoldi restarts, and recursive least squares.

Takeaway: Householder vs Givens is a mechanism question (n−1 zeros vs 1 zero), not a taste question; and mirrors scale up through the WY block form and down into the group structure — every rotation is two reflections, every orthogonal matrix is a QR.

9. Everything on one screen

The recipe. To zero \(\boldsymbol{x}\)'s tail: \(\;\alpha=-\operatorname{sign}(x_1)\|\boldsymbol{x}\|,\;\;\boldsymbol{v}=\boldsymbol{x}-\alpha e_1,\;\; H=I-\beta\boldsymbol{v}\boldsymbol{v}^{\mathsf T},\;\;\beta=2/\boldsymbol{v}^{\mathsf T}\boldsymbol{v}\), apply as \(\boldsymbol{x}\leftarrow\boldsymbol{x}-\beta\boldsymbol{v}(\boldsymbol{v}^{\mathsf T}\boldsymbol{x})\); \(\gamma=1\) on fresh reflectors (because \(\boldsymbol{v}^{\mathsf T}\boldsymbol{x}=\tfrac12\boldsymbol{v}^{\mathsf T}\boldsymbol{v}\)); store \((\hat{\boldsymbol{v}},\tau)\), \(\tau=1-x_1/\alpha\).

And the reason Figure 1's blue line is flat: a Householder QR cannot produce a non-orthogonal \(Q\) even when its parameters are garbage — the structure is the stability.
