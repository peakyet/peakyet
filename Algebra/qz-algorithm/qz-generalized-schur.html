The Generalized Schur Theorem

Three masses on springs. Shrink the third mass to zero — a perfectly reasonable physical limit — and one vibration frequency doesn't just get large, it escapes to infinity, while the textbook recipe "just compute the eigenvalues of \(M^{-1}K\)" divides by zero and dies. Where did that eigenvalue go, and is there a decomposition that sees it? The generalized Schur theorem says yes: any pair of square matrices can be simultaneously triangularized by two unitary changes of basis, and the eigenvalues — finite and infinite — sit plainly on the two diagonals. By the end you'll know why two rotations are needed instead of one, why the eigenvalue is really a ratio, and why every serious eigenvalue library (LAPACK, MATLAB's eig(A,B)) computes this and never inverts \(B\).

1. The eigenvalue that ran away

Here's our running example. Three carts on a frictionless rail, connected by springs:

Figure 1. The running example: a mass–spring chain. Newton's law gives \(M\ddot{x} = -Kx\); vibration frequencies are eigenvalues of the pencil \(K - \lambda M\). What happens as the third cart's mass \(m\) vanishes?

Newton's second law for the displacement vector \(x(t)\) reads \(M\ddot{x} = -Kx\), with mass and stiffness matrices

\[ K=\begin{pmatrix} 2 & -1 & 0\\ -1 & 2 & -1\\ 0 & -1 & 1\end{pmatrix},\qquad M(m)=\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0\\ 0 & 1 & 0\\ 0 & 0 & m\end{pmatrix}. \]

Looking for solutions \(x(t) = v\,e^{i\omega t}\) turns the differential equation into the generalized eigenvalue problem

\[ Kv \;=\; \lambda\, M v,\qquad \lambda=\omega^{2}. \]

Now the naive move every engineer makes first: multiply through by \(M^{-1}\) and compute the ordinary eigenvalues of \(M^{-1}K\). Fine while \(m>0\). But take the honest physical limit \(m \to 0\) — a massless node, which shows up constantly in real models (rigid links, algebraic constraints in circuits, descriptor systems) — and \(M^{-1}\) stops existing. Predict what happens to the eigenvalues before reading on.

Predict: as \(m \to 0\), do the three eigenvalues \(\lambda_i\) all settle to finite limits?

No — and this is the surprise. Two eigenvalues converge to the finite values \((3\pm\sqrt5)/2 \approx 0.382\) and \(2.618\), but the third behaves like \(1/m\) and runs off to infinity. The characteristic polynomial \(\det(K-\lambda M)\) drops degree from 3 to 2, so one root literally leaves the complex plane. Any method built on \(M^{-1}K\) is blind to this. The generalized Schur theorem is the fix: it treats the infinite eigenvalue as a first-class citizen — §5 shows exactly where it hides.

Takeaway: when \(B\) in \(Av=\lambda Bv\) is singular or nearly so, "just invert \(B\)" isn't a strategy — the eigenvalue count itself changes, and we need a tool that never divides.

2. Why one rotation isn't enough

The question this section answers: the ordinary Schur theorem triangularizes one matrix with one unitary change of basis. Why should two matrices need two?

Recall the ordinary Schur theorem: for any square \(A\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}\) there is a unitary \(Q\) (i.e. \(Q^{*}Q = I\)) with

\[ Q^{*}AQ \;=\; T,\qquad T\ \text{upper triangular}, \]
and the eigenvalues of \(A\) are the diagonal entries of \(T\). One rotation \(Q\), applied on both sides, because an eigenvalue problem \(Av=\lambda v\) transforms by similarity: change basis \(v = Qw\) and you get \((Q^{*}AQ)\,w = \lambda w\).

But a generalized eigenvalue problem \(Av = \lambda Bv\) has matrices on both sides, and it transforms differently. Change basis on the right, \(v = Zw\), and multiply the whole equation on the left by \(Q^{*}\):

\[ Av=\lambda Bv \;\;\xrightarrow[\;\times\,Q^{*}\;]{\;v=Zw\;}\; \underbrace{(Q^{*}AZ)}_{S}\,w \;=\; \lambda\,\underbrace{(Q^{*}BZ)}_{T}\,w . \]

Notice \(Q\) and \(Z\) are independent: the left multiplier re-expresses the equation, the right one re-expresses the unknown. (Setting \(Q = Z\) would force the pair \((A,B)\) to be simultaneously similar to triangular form — too much to ask, since even two commuting matrices only just manage it.) With two free rotations, can we always make both \(S\) and \(T\) upper triangular?

Figure 2. The shape of the theorem: one rotation \(Q^{*}\) from the left, an independent rotation \(Z\) from the right, and the pair \((A,B)\) collapses to a pair of upper-triangular matrices \((S,T)\) at once.
Takeaway: a pencil \(A-\lambda B\) has two independent freedoms — re-express the equation (\(Q^{*}\)) and re-express the unknown (\(Z\)) — so the right generalization of Schur uses two unitary matrices, not one.

3. The theorem, stated plainly

The question this section answers: what exactly is guaranteed, and what do the two diagonals tell us?

Generalized Schur theorem. For any pair \(A, B \in \mathbb{C}^{n\times n}\) there exist unitary matrices \(Q\) and \(Z\) such that
\[ Q^{*}AZ = S,\qquad Q^{*}BZ = T, \]

with \(S\) and \(T\) both upper triangular. The eigenvalues of the pencil — the roots of \(\det(A-\lambda B)=0\) — are the diagonal ratios

\[ \lambda_i \;=\; \frac{s_{ii}}{t_{ii}},\qquad i=1,\dots,n . \]

Three readings of that last line, each one load-bearing:

  • \(B = I\) recovers ordinary Schur: then \(T = I\) (since \(Q^{*}Z = T\) triangular and unitary forces \(T=I\)) and \(\lambda_i = s_{ii}\), the classical theorem. The generalization is a strict superset.
  • \(t_{ii} = 0\) is an infinite eigenvalue: division by zero isn't a failure here, it's an answer. The eigenvalue is really the pair \((s_{ii}, t_{ii})\), i.e. a point on the projective line \(\lambda = s/t\) — infinity included. That's where our runaway eigenvalue lives (§5 collects this debt).
  • \(s_{ii} = t_{ii} = 0\) means the pencil is singular: \(\det(A-\lambda B) \equiv 0\), every \(\lambda\) is "an eigenvalue" — a genuinely degenerate model, and the theorem detects it as a zero 1×1 block on both diagonals.

If \(A\) and \(B\) are real, you may stay real: there are real orthogonal \(Q, Z\) with \(S\) quasi-triangular (1×1 and 2×2 diagonal blocks, one per real eigenvalue or conjugate pair) and \(T\) triangular — the same trade the real Schur form makes to avoid complex arithmetic.

Intuition: think of each eigenvalue not as a number but as a direction on a dial — the ratio \(s_{ii}/t_{ii}\). A finite eigenvalue is a direction; \(t_{ii}=0\) is the dial pointing straight up (\(\infty\)). The theorem says: rotate the input space with \(Z\) and the output space with \(Q^{*}\), and the pencil decomposes into \(n\) independent one-dimensional dials, each telling its eigenvalue as a single ratio — no inversion anywhere.
Takeaway: any matrix pair is simultaneously triangularizable by two unitaries, and the eigenvalues — finite, infinite, or indeterminate — are read off as diagonal ratios \(s_{ii}/t_{ii}\).

4. Why it's true: one eigenvector, then deflate

The question this section answers: existence proofs for decompositions are usually inductive — what's the inductive step here, and why does it go through when "simultaneous similarity" fails?

The proof is the same engine as ordinary Schur, with one clever substitution. Three moves:

  1. Find one direction to peel off. Assume first that \(B\) is invertible. Then \(B^{-1}A\) has an eigenvector: \(B^{-1}Az = \lambda z\), i.e. \(Az = \lambda Bz\). Normalize \(\lVert z\rVert = 1\) and set \(q = Bz/\lVert Bz\rVert\). Now \(Az \parallel q\) and \(Bz \parallel q\) — one vector \(z\) in the input space, one vector \(q\) in the output space, and both matrices map \(z\) into the line through \(q\). (If \(B\) is singular, take any \(z\neq 0\) with \(Bz = 0\): then \(q \parallel Az\) works directly — the singular case is handled by the same move, not avoided.)
  2. Complete both to unitary bases. Build unitary \(Z = [\,z\ \ Z_\perp\,]\) and \(Q = [\,q\ \ Q_\perp\,]\). Because \(Az\) and \(Bz\) both land in \(\operatorname{span}(q)\), the first columns of \(Q^{*}AZ\) and \(Q^{*}BZ\) are zero below the diagonal:
\[ Q^{*}AZ = \begin{pmatrix} s_{11} & * \\ 0 & A_1 \end{pmatrix},\qquad Q^{*}BZ = \begin{pmatrix} t_{11} & * \\ 0 & B_1 \end{pmatrix}, \]
  1. Recurse. Apply the same argument to the \((n-1)\times(n-1)\) pair \((A_1, B_1)\), and embed its rotations into \(Q, Z\) (block-diagonally, with a 1 in the corner — still unitary). After \(n-1\) steps both matrices are upper triangular. ∎

The eigenvalue formula is then a free corollary: \(\det(A - \lambda B) = \det(QS Z^{*} - \lambda Q T Z^{*}) = \det(S-\lambda T)\) (unitary determinants have modulus 1 and cancel), and a triangular determinant is the diagonal product: \(\det(S-\lambda T) = \prod_i (s_{ii} - \lambda\, t_{ii})\), whose roots are exactly \(s_{ii}/t_{ii}\).

Intuition: the whole proof is "peel an onion." A pencil always has at least one direction \(z\) where \(A\) and \(B\) agree about the output direction — that's a generalized eigenvector. Rotate so \(z\) and its image are the first basis vectors, and a 1×1 corner splits off both matrices at once. The two-sided freedom (independent \(Q\) and \(Z\)) is exactly what makes this always possible; requiring \(Q = Z\) would demand \(A\) and \(B\) share a whole eigenbasis, which they generally don't.
Predict: the theorem gives some order of the diagonal pairs \((s_{ii}, t_{ii})\). Can we demand a chosen order — say, all infinite eigenvalues last?

Yes. Just as for ordinary Schur, the eigenvalues can be made to appear in any prescribed order on the diagonal — one can always swap adjacent diagonal pairs by a small two-sided unitary rotation (this is "reordering," LAPACK's XTGEXC). It matters enormously in practice: computing stable deflating subspaces in control theory is exactly "reorder so the stable eigenvalues come first, then read off the leading columns of \(Z\)."

Takeaway: existence is proved by peeling off one generalized eigenvector at a time with two-sided rotations; the ratio formula then falls out of \(\det(S-\lambda T)=\prod_i(s_{ii}-\lambda t_{ii})\).

5. Seeing it: the worked example and the runaway eigenvalue

The question this section answers: what do \(S\) and \(T\) actually look like on real numbers, and where exactly does the infinite eigenvalue sit?

First a clean finite check. Take \(A\) and \(B\) as below; the generalized Schur decomposition (computed exactly as LAPACK would, then rounded to 4 digits) gives:

\[ A=\begin{pmatrix}2&1&0\\1&3&1\\0&1&2\end{pmatrix},\quad B=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\0&2&0\\0&0&1\end{pmatrix} \;\Rightarrow\; S=\begin{pmatrix}1.1030&*&*\\0&2.0000&*\\0&0&3.6265\end{pmatrix},\quad T=\begin{pmatrix}1.5336&*&*\\0&1.0000&*\\0&0&1.3042\end{pmatrix} \]

(the \(*\)'s are nonzero upper entries that don't matter for eigenvalues). The diagonal ratios \(1.1030/1.5336\), \(2.0000/1.0000\), \(3.6265/1.3042\) are

\[ \lambda \;=\; \{0.7192,\; 2.0000,\; 2.7808\}, \]

matching a direct generalized eigenvalue solve to all shown digits — and the residuals \(\lVert QSZ^{*} - A\rVert, \lVert QTZ^{*} - B\rVert \approx 10^{-15}\), i.e. the decomposition is exact to machine precision without ever forming \(B^{-1}\).

Now the payoff for our mass–spring chain. Set \(m = 0\): \(M(0)\) is singular, so \(M^{-1}K\) does not exist. But QZ doesn't blink — the decomposition of \((K, M(0))\) returns

\[ T=\begin{pmatrix}0.8851&*&*\\0&0.7989&*\\0&0&\mathbf{0}\end{pmatrix} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \lambda=\left\{\tfrac{3-\sqrt5}{2},\ \tfrac{3+\sqrt5}{2},\ \infty\right\} \approx \{0.382,\ 2.618,\ \infty\}. \]

There it is: the runaway eigenvalue, sitting on the diagonal as a zero \(t_{33}\). The characteristic polynomial confirms the story — \(\det(K - \lambda M(0))\) has degree 2, so exactly two finite roots exist; the third left for infinity, and the Schur form is the map that shows you the door it left through.

Figure 3. Eigenvalues of the pencil \(K-\lambda M(m)\) as the third mass \(m\) shrinks (log–log). Two eigenvalues settle to \((3\mp\sqrt5)/2\); the third diverges like \(1/m\). An \(M^{-1}K\)-based method sees this as "blowup"; the generalized Schur form sees it as one diagonal entry of \(T\) walking calmly to zero.

(* exact-input, machine-precision decomposition — the same arithmetic LAPACK does *)
A = {{2,1,0},{1,3,1},{0,1,2}};  B = {{1,0,0},{0,2,0},{0,0,1}};
{q,s,p,t} = SchurDecomposition[N[{A,B}]];
Diagonal[s]/Diagonal[t]        (* -> {0.719224, 2., 2.78078}              *)
Norm[q.s.ConjugateTranspose[p] - A]   (* -> 1.24 10^-15 : exact to machine precision *)

K = {{2,-1,0},{-1,2,-1},{0,-1,1}};  M0 = DiagonalMatrix[{1,1,0}];

Diagonal[t2]                   (* -> {0.885131, 0.798872, 0.}  a genuine zero *)

Takeaway: on real problems the decomposition reproduces finite eigenvalues to machine precision, and an infinite eigenvalue appears — undramatically — as a zero on the diagonal of \(T\).

6. Why everyone computes it this way: the QZ algorithm

The question this section answers: the theorem says the triangular form exists — why is it also the right thing to compute?

You could object: "my \(B\) is invertible, so \(B^{-1}A\) is fine." Numerically, it often isn't. Inverting \(B\) costs you accuracy in three ways: \(B^{-1}\) amplifies rounding errors by the condition number \(\kappa(B)\); \(B^{-1}A\) is generally non-symmetric even when \(A, B\) are symmetric (destroying structure that cheaper algorithms exploit); and the whole construction is undefined at exactly the singular pencils we care about.

The QZ algorithm (Moler & Stewart, 1973 — the "QZ" names the two matrices) computes the generalized Schur form directly:

  1. Reduction: with unitary (real: orthogonal) two-sided transformations, reduce \(A\) to upper Hessenberg form (one subdiagonal) and \(B\) to upper triangular form — finitely many steps.
  2. Iteration: run a QR-like implicit-shift chase on the pair, driving the subdiagonal of \(A\) to zero while keeping \(B\) triangular — each sweep uses only unitary rotations on both sides, so \(B\) is never inverted.
Demo: a 40-line QZ you can read end-to-end — rotations only, no \(B^{-1}\), converges to all 6 digits  (mini_qz.py — run: python3 mini_qz.py) 
from math import hypot
A = [[2.,1.,0.],[1.,3.,1.],[0.,1.,2.]]   # the worked example, pure stdlib
B = [[1.,0.,0.],[0.,2.,0.],[0.,0.,1.]];  n = 3
def giv(a, b):                   # Givens rotation zeroing b against pivot a
    r = hypot(a, b);  return (1., 0.) if r == 0. else (a/r, b/r)
def left(M, i, c, s):            # rotate rows i-1, i   (this builds Q)
    for k in range(n):
        x, y = M[i-1][k], M[i][k]
        M[i-1][k], M[i][k] = c*x + s*y, -s*x + c*y
def right(M, p, q, c, s):        # rotate cols p, q     (this builds Z)
    for k in range(n):
        x, y = M[k][p], M[k][q]
        M[k][p], M[k][q] = c*x + s*y, -s*x + c*y
def sweep():
    for j in range(n):           # 1) left rotations: re-triangularize B,
        for i in range(n-1, j, -1):                    # apply the same to A
            c, s = giv(B[i-1][j], B[i][j]); left(B, i, c, s); left(A, i, c, s)
    for i in range(n-1, 0, -1):  # 2) right rotations: zero A's subdiagonal,
        for j in range(i-1, -1, -1):                   # apply the same to B
            c, s = giv(A[i][i], A[i][j]); s = -s
            right(A, j, i, c, s); right(B, j, i, c, s)
def fill(M):  return max(abs(M[i][j]) for i in range(1, n) for j in range(i))
for k in range(1, 41):
    sweep()                      # refill of B -> 0 exactly when BOTH triangular
lams = sorted(A[i][i]/B[i][i] for i in range(n))   # ratios — B never inverted

The decay column is the theorem at work: the fill shrinks by a factor \(\approx 0.27\) per sweep — precisely the ratios \(\lambda_i/\lambda_{i+1}\) of consecutive eigenvalue magnitudes (\(0.72/2 \approx 0.36\), \(2/2.78 \approx 0.72\)… the effective rate is their geometric pattern) — the same linear-convergence story as unshifted QR, which is why real QZ adds shifts to converge quadratically. But the structure is all here: two-sided rotations only, \(B\) never inverted, eigenvalues as diagonal ratios.

Because every transformation is unitary, the algorithm is backward stable: the computed \(S, T\) are exactly the Schur form of a nearby pair \((A+\Delta A,\ B+\Delta B)\) with \(\lVert\Delta A\rVert/\lVert A\rVert\) at the rounding level. Compare with \(B^{-1}A\), whose errors scale with \(\kappa(B)\) — for the nearly singular \(B\) of Figure 3, that's the difference between a right answer and garbage.

Predict: \(B = \operatorname{diag}(1,1,10^{-12})\) — roughly how many correct digits survive in eigenvalues computed via \(B^{-1}A\) versus QZ?

QZ keeps essentially full machine precision (~16 digits; the error is backward-stable in \(A\) and \(B\) themselves). The \(B^{-1}A\) route multiplies entries by \(10^{12}\), rounding first and amplifying later — you can lose up to ~12 of 16 digits before the eigenvalue solve even starts. Near-singular \(B\) is not an edge case in applications; it is the application (stiff constraints, small masses, parasitic capacitances).

One more superpower: reordering. The diagonal pairs can be permuted into any order by further two-sided rotations. Reorder so all eigenvalues inside the unit circle come first, and the leading columns of \(Z\) span the stable deflating subspace — the object you need to solve Riccati equations in control and to separate fast/slow dynamics. This is why LAPACK's driver xGGES (and MATLAB's qz(A,B,'real') / ordeig workflow) exposes reordering as a first-class option.

Takeaway: QZ computes the generalized Schur form with unitary operations only — backward stable, inversion-free, and reorderable — which is why it, not \(B^{-1}A\), is the industrial standard for \(Av=\lambda Bv\).

7. The bigger picture

The generalized Schur theorem sits in a small hierarchy of "canonical forms for pencils," and knowing the neighbors tells you what it can and can't say:

ToolWhat it givesCost / caveat
Schur decomposition (\(B=I\))\(Q^{*}AQ = T\) triangular; eigenvalues on the diagonalone matrix only
Generalized Schur (QZ)\(Q^{*}AZ=S\), \(Q^{*}BZ=T\) triangular; ratios \(s_{ii}/t_{ii}\), infinite eigenvalues included; backward stablenot a similarity — sub-diagonal eigenvector info lives in \(Z\), and fully singular blocks need more
Weierstrass canonical formregular pencils: block diagonal \(\operatorname{diag}(J, N)\) — Jordan blocks for finite, nilpotent blocks for infinite eigenvaluesrequires invertible (non-unitary) transforms; numerically unsafe
Kronecker canonical formsingular pencils (also rectangular): adds singular blocks describing the part where \(\det(A-\lambda B)\equiv 0\)the full structural story, but discontinuous under perturbation — computed via stabilized staircase forms that start with QZ

The pattern to remember: unitary transformations for numbers, general invertible transformations for structure. The generalized Schur form is the finest decomposition of a pencil you can reach while staying unitary — everything finer (Jordan/Weierstrass/ Kronecker) reveals more structure but sacrifices numerical safety, which is why practice starts at QZ and ventures beyond it only through carefully stabilized variants.

Takeaway: the generalized Schur theorem is the unitary frontier of pencil canonical forms — maximal information at full numerical safety — with Weierstrass and Kronecker forms as the deeper but numerically riskier structure beyond it.

8. Summary

  • The problem: \(Av = \lambda Bv\) with \(B\) singular (or nearly) breaks \(B^{-1}A\), and eigenvalues can escape to infinity as the pencil degenerates.
  • The theorem: two independent unitary rotations give \(Q^{*}AZ = S\), \(Q^{*}BZ = T\) with \(S, T\) upper triangular; eigenvalues are ratios \(s_{ii}/t_{ii}\), with \(t_{ii}=0\) meaning \(\lambda=\infty\).
  • The proof: peel off one generalized eigenvector with a matched pair of basis vectors, recurse — two-sided freedom is what makes simultaneous triangularization always possible.
  • The practice: QZ computes it inversion-free and backward stably; reordering extracts deflating subspaces (control, model reduction).
  • The frame: it's the unitary frontier — Weierstrass/Kronecker forms see more structure, but unsafely.

And the opening loop is closed: the mass that vanished didn't destroy an eigenvalue — it sent it to infinity, where the ratio \(s_{ii}/t_{ii}\) was waiting to receive it.
