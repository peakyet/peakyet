Section 1

Two eigenvalue problems

Question this section answers

What is an eigenvalue algorithm allowed to do to a matrix — and what changes when a \(B\) appears in \(Ax=\lambda Bx\)?

“Compute the eigenvalues” has three tempting answers Running example: \(C=\mathrm{circ}(1,2,3,4)\), DFT-diagonalized: \(\lambda\in\{10,\,-2,\,-2\pm 2i\}\).

  • Characteristic polynomial. \(\lambda^4-4\lambda^3-44\lambda^2-144\lambda-160\) — but this swaps a matrix problem for a root problem.
  • Power iteration from \((1,2,4,7)\) walks to \(\lambda=10\) at ratio \(0.078\approx(2.83/10)^2\) — but yields one eigenvalue of \(n\).
  • Sensitivity is a ceiling: \(\begin{bmatrix}1&1000\\10^{-16}&1\end{bmatrix}\) has eigenvalues \(1\pm 3.16\cdot 10^{-7}\).

What we want

All eigenvalues, from the matrix, backward stable, \(O(n^3)\) total — and the same for a pencil.

A pencil: \(Ax=\lambda Bx\), and why \(B^{-1}A\) is wrong

Roots of \(\det(A-\lambda B)=0\), often with singular \(B\). Smallest example that breaks:

\[ A=\begin{bmatrix}2&0\\0&1\end{bmatrix},\quad B=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\0&0\end{bmatrix},\quad \det(A-\lambda B)=2-\lambda. \]

Predict

How many finite eigenvalues? Does \(B^{-1}A\) exist?

  • One finite (\(\lambda=2\)) and one at infinity: \(\deg\det\) dropped below \(n=2\), and \(B^{-1}A\) does not exist.

Never form \(B^{-1}\) — \((A,10^{-16}B)\) keeps the eigenvalues and scales \(B^{-1}A\) by \(10^{16}\). The object is the pair.

The running pencil, and the two target forms

Act 1: \(C\) alone. Act 2: the pencil \((C,B)\) with \(B=3I+\tfrac12 C\). Both share the DFT eigenvectors, so \(\mu=\lambda/(3+\lambda/2)\) gives the answer in closed form:

\[ \tfrac{10}{8}=\tfrac54,\quad \tfrac{-2}{2}=-1,\quad \tfrac{-2\pm 2i}{2\pm i}=\tfrac{-2\pm 6i}{5}. \]
  • Two real eigenvalues and one complex pair — so both block kinds must appear in the answer.

Target

Block-triangular in orthogonal coordinates: real Schur form for \(C\); generalized real Schur \((S,T)\) for the pencil.

The promise: orthogonal moves only

  • Standard problem: similarity \(C\mapsto Q^\top C Q\) with \(Q\) orthogonal — \(\det(C-\lambda I)\) is untouched.
  • Pencil: one equivalence \((A,B)\mapsto (U^\top A V,\ U^\top B V)\) — the same \(U,V\) for both matrices; \(\det U^\top\det V\neq 0\) only rescales \(\det(A-\lambda B)\).
  • Backward stability comes free: orthogonal factors never amplify error — measured residuals \(\sim 10^{-15}\) per transformation.

The cost contract

Pay \(O(n^3)\) once to reduce to a banded shape; every later iteration must cost \(O(n^2)\). Section 2 finds that shape.

Section 2

Reduce once: Hessenberg and Hessenberg–triangular form

Question this section answers

Each iteration must touch \(O(n^2)\) entries, not \(O(n^2)\) rows of dense work. What shape makes a sweep cheap — for one matrix, and for a pair?

Hessenberg form: pay \(O(n^3)\) once Householder similarity, as in the companion note §6–§8: \(Q^\top C Q=H_0\), zeros below the first subdiagonal.

     [  0       3.112  -1.385   1.054 ]
     [  0       0      -1.174  -1.994 ]

|h(i+1,i)| = 5.385, 3.112, 1.174
similarity defect 2.2e−15 · trace 4

  • One column of \(H\) has two nonzeros — so \(p(H)\boldsymbol e_1\) for a quadratic \(p\) costs \(O(1)\) and has three entries.
  • A sweep is \(n-1\) Givens rotations, each touching \(O(n)\) entries — \(O(n^2)\) per sweep.
  • Reduction: \(O(n^3)\), paid once. Eigenvalues untouched: \(10,\,-2,\,-2\pm 2i\).

For a pair: Hessenberg–triangular form

Find orthogonal \(U,V\) with \(U^\top A V=H\) Hessenberg and \(U^\top B V=T\) triangular — two phases, no inversion (Moler–Stewart 1973):

  • Phase 1: QR-factor \(B\) from the left only: \(U_1^\top B=T\), \(A\mapsto U_1^\top A\). Singular \(B\) is fine — \(R\) carries zero pivots.
  • Phase 2: Hessenbergize \(A\) with row mixes on \((A,T)\); each dirties one entry of \(T\), and a column mix restores it. Residuals on our pencil: \(8.9\cdot10^{-16}\) and \(1.3\cdot10^{-15}\).

Why triangular \(T\)

A zero on \(T\)'s diagonal is an infinite eigenvalue, visible at a glance — and triangular solves with \(T\) give the step its cheap starting vector.

Section 3

The Francis QR step

Question this section answers

Shifts make the iteration converge, but a complex pair wants a complex shift — or a double shift in real arithmetic. How do you apply \(p(H)\) without forming it?

Unshifted QR: power iteration on a whole basis Sweep: \(H_{k+1}=R_kQ_k=Q_k^\top H_kQ_k\) — simultaneous iteration on all of \(\mathbb{R}^n\) (Watkins 1982).

\(\lvert h(2,1)\rvert\)0.2820.283

after 200 sweeps: |h(2,1)|=2e−44, |h(4,3)|=1e−30, |h(3,2)|=2.000000

  • Each subdiagonal decays at the ratio of adjacent moduli — measured over sweeps 2–12.
  • Linear convergence: 88 sweeps to split \((2,1)\) and \((4,3)\).

Defect 1: rate

Shifts attack the constant — but one subdiagonal refuses to move (next frame).

The subdiagonal that will not deflate

Predict

\(|h(3,2)|\) has sat at \(2.000\) for 200 sweeps. Is 200 simply too few — or is something structural going on?

  • Structural: \(\lambda_2,\lambda_3=-2\pm 2i\) have equal modulus, so the predicted rate is exactly \(1\). No decay is possible.
  • And it is not failing to converge — it converged to \(2.000\), the subdiagonal of the pair’s \(2\times2\) block. Zeroing it would split a complex pair.

Defect 2: structure

Accept \(2\times2\) blocks as answers — the real Schur form. With the rate fix, that is §3’s target.

Shifts — and why one is not enough

  • Shifted step: \(H-\sigma I=QR\), \(H'=RQ+\sigma I\). Measured: real Rayleigh shift \(\sigma=h_{nn}\) needs 19 sweeps, not 88.
  • But our pair is complex. A real \(\sigma\) cannot approach \(-2\pm 2i\); a complex \(\sigma\) drags every entry into \(\mathbb{C}\).
  • Francis’s answer: shift by the pair at once. \(p(x)=(x-\sigma)(x-\bar\sigma)=x^2-sx+t\) with \(s,t\) real. From the trailing \(2\times2\) of \(H_0\): \(\sigma=-1.6897\pm 1.0701\,i\), \(s=-3.3793\), \(t=4.0\).

The deal

Two shifts for the price of one real sweep: apply \(p\) by a similarity — and converge in 10 sweeps on our example.

The Implicit Q theorem — the key

Implicit Q theorem. Let \(H\) be upper Hessenberg with nonzero subdiagonals. If \(Q_1^\top H Q_1\) and \(Q_2^\top H Q_2\) are both upper Hessenberg and \(Q_1\boldsymbol e_1=\pm Q_2\boldsymbol e_1\), then \(Q_1=Q_2D\) for a diagonal \(D\) of \(\pm1\) signs.
  • Punchline: the first column of \(Q\) fixes all of \(Q\) up to signs — so \(H'=Q^\top H Q\) is pinned by one vector, \(\boldsymbol v=p(H)\boldsymbol e_1\).
  • Hessenberg zeros make \(\boldsymbol v\) cheap: \(H\boldsymbol e_1\) has 2 entries, \(H^2\boldsymbol e_1\) has 3. For \(H_0\): \((33.379,\,-57.937,\,-16.758,\,0)\) — entry 4 vanishes exactly.

Predict

\(p(H)\) is \(n\times n\). Which part of it does the Francis step need to build \(Q\)?

The Francis step: three entries, one bulge

  • Answer: only \(p(H)\boldsymbol e_1\) — three numbers. Build the Householder \(V_1\) with \(V_1\boldsymbol e_1\parallel\boldsymbol v\), then \(H\leftarrow V_1^\top H V_1\).
  • Honest but incomplete: \(H\) is Hessenberg plus one bulge of fill below the band — here at \((3,1),(4,1),(4,2)\).
  • Implicit \(=\) explicit, up to signs: \(\|D\,H_{\mathrm{exp}}D-H_{\mathrm{imp}}\|_F=8.2\cdot10^{-15}\), and \(Q\boldsymbol e_1\parallel p(H)\boldsymbol e_1\) to \(3.6\cdot10^{-15}\).

Why implicit wins

Forming \(p(H)=H^2-sH+tI\) costs \(\Theta(n^3)\) — and we needed three numbers from it. Measured per double step at \(n=64\): 2.6M ops explicit vs 161k implicit (\(n^3\) vs \(n^2\); verify_qz_algorithm.py).

Chasing the bulge — the signature move

  • The chase: reflector on the 3 rows of the bulge column, applied from both sides — the fill walks down and exits bottom-right.
  • Measured walk on \(H_0\): \(\{(3,1),(4,1),(4,2)\}\to\{(4,2)\}\to\varnothing\). Each stage \(O(n)\); \(O(n)\) stages: \(O(n^2)\) per double step.
  • Production exactly: LAPACK’s 3×3 DLARFG sweep plus a Givens finish (dlahqr, dhgeqz).

Build \(Q\) from \(p(H)\boldsymbol e_1\), chase the bulge: two real shifts, \(O(n^2)\), no \(\sqrt{-1}\).

Section 4

The QZ step

Question this section answers

A left reflector cleans \(H\)’s bulge and breaks \(T\)’s triangularity. How does the chase survive on a pencil?

What changes when \(B\neq I\)

  • The move is a paired equivalence: \(H'=Q^\top H Z\), \(T'=Q^\top T Z\) — one \(Q\) on the left of both, one \(Z\) on the right of both.
  • The engine is a QR step on \(M=HT^{-1}\) — never formed. Shifts are the trailing \(2\times2\) pencil’s eigenvalues (\(\mu=-0.2463\pm 0.2149\,i\) here).
  • The starting vector \(p(M)\boldsymbol e_1\): two triangular solves \(T\boldsymbol w=\boldsymbol e_1\), \(T\boldsymbol y=M\boldsymbol e_1\) — still 3 entries. No inversion of \(B\) or \(T\).

Predict

The first left reflector zeros \(H\)’s bulge. What did it do to \(T\)?

Left, right, left: the alternating chase

  • Answer: it mixed two rows of \(T\), putting one entry under \(T\)’s diagonal. \(H\)-bulge traded for a \(T\)-bulge.
  • Right reflector (from \(T\)’s 3×3 block): restores \(T\), drops \(H\)’s bulge one row. Then the next left reflector.
  • Givens pair for the last two columns, as in LAPACK dhgeqz. Subdiagonals: \((3.658,\,3.300,\,2.178)\to(3.018,\,3.067,\,1.060)\).

Structure survives

Off-structure entries \(\sim 2\cdot10^{-16}\); backward errors \(1.8\cdot10^{-15}\) and \(8.9\cdot10^{-16}\); eigenvalues unchanged.

The chase on \((H,T)\), stage by stage

Deflation, \(\infty\)-eigenvalues, and the result

  • Deflate when \(|H(i+1,i)|\le \varepsilon(|H(i,i)|+|H(i+1,i+1)|)\): zero it, split, continue per block. A complex \(2\times2\) block is a legitimate answer.
  • Infinite eigenvalues surface as \(T(i,i)\to 0\) — treated explicitly by Ward 1975 and Kågström–Kressner 2007.

Section 5

Cost, limits, connections, sources

Question this section answers

What does a production eigensolver add to this core loop — and where is the whole machinery actually used?

What production adds — and what it cannot

Added around the core loop

  • Better shifts: Wilkinson-style trailing \(2\times2\) pencil shifts; Ward’s combination shifts (1975); exceptional shifts after stagnation — see the “Exceptional shift” block in dlahqr.
  • Multishift + aggressive early deflation (Kågström–Kressner 2007): chase many small bulges, reach BLAS-3, deflate early.
  • Blocked HT reduction (Bujanović–Karlsson–Kressner 2018): Householder blocks for parallel machines.

Limits of the guarantee

  • Backward stable, not forward magic: residuals \(\sim\varepsilon\|A\|\), but the Jordan demo shows the data can still be ill-conditioned.
  • Global convergence on Hessenberg form is subtle — unlike symmetric tridiagonal (Wilkinson 1968), where every shift works. Watkins 2008 is the modern route in.
  • Singular structure needs explicit handling: \(\infty\)-eigenvalues and singular pencils want the deflation logic of Ward 1975 / Kågström–Kressner 2007.

Connections: where this machinery runs

  • LAPACK. dgehrd/dgghrd reduce; dlahqr/dhseqr run Francis QR; dhgeqz runs the QZ sweep you just watched.
  • Control. Generalized eigenvalues of Hamiltonian and descriptor pencils are the currency of optimal control — see \(H_2\) control, LMIs, interior-point methods.
  • Elimination primitives. The reflectors and rotations of the reductions come from Householder transformations.

The whole recipe, in one line

Reduce to (Hessenberg) / (Hessenberg, triangular), then chase a degree-2 bulge with orthogonal reflectors — never forming \(p(H)\) or \(HT^{-1}\), never inverting \(B\), never leaving the reals.
