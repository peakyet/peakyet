The QZ Algorithm and the Algebraic Riccati Equation

A quadratic matrix equation with several answers, a plant whose modes you cannot decouple, and one pencil whose invariant planes are the answers: how the generalized Schur decomposition turns "which root?" into "which subspace?", and where that stops being enough.

1. Which of the four candidates is the gain?

Question: The LQR gain comes from one matrix equation, and that equation is quadratic in its unknown, so it has several answers. The plant below is unstable. What decides which answer is the right one, and what kind of object do we actually have to compute?

The plant is \(x_{k+1} = A x_k + B u_k\) and the cost per step is \(x_k' Q x_k + u_k' R u_k\), with

\[ A = \begin{pmatrix} \tfrac{1}{2} & 1 \\ 1 & \tfrac{1}{2} \end{pmatrix}, \qquad B = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad Q = \begin{pmatrix} \tfrac{1}{2} & -\tfrac{1}{4} \\ -\tfrac{1}{4} & \tfrac{1}{2} \end{pmatrix}, \qquad R = [\,1\,]. \]

Two features of these numbers matter already. The open loop has eigenvalues \(1.5\) and \(-0.5\), so one mode grows by fifty percent per step: feedback is not a refinement here, it is what keeps the state bounded. And \(Q\) has an off-diagonal entry, so the two states are coupled in the cost: there is no change of coordinates that makes this two independent scalar problems. The quantity to find is the symmetric solution \(X\) of the discrete algebraic Riccati equation, and the gain it defines.

The equation to be solved
\[ X = Q + A'XA - A'XB\,(R + B'XB)^{-1}B'XA, \qquad K = (R + B'XB)^{-1}B'XA. \]

Solve for \(X\), form \(K\), and the closed loop is \(A - BK\). The catch is in the word "the". The equation is quadratic: with \(n = 1\) it is an ordinary quadratic with two roots, and with \(n = 2\) it has several symmetric solutions. So "solve the Riccati equation" is really a selection problem, and a selection needs a rule. The rule is not size. It is this: the solution the LQR gain comes from is the one whose closed loop \(A - BK\) has every multiplier inside the unit disk, and that solution is called the stabilizing one.

Intuition: A quadratic equation in a matrix unknown does not have "roots" in an ordered sense; it has a finite family of candidate subspaces, and only some of them are graphs of a matrix. Whatever computes the answer should therefore work with subspaces, not with numbers to be compared.

The answer we are chasing, stated now so that this page can be read straight through: for this plant the stabilizing solution is \(P = I\), the gain is \(K = [\,\tfrac12 \;\; \tfrac12\,]\), the closed loop is \(A - BK = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & \tfrac12 \\ \tfrac12 & 0 \end{pmatrix}\) with multipliers \(\pm\tfrac12\), and in exact rational arithmetic the residual of the equation above at \(X = P\) is exactly \(0\). What follows is about reaching that answer by a method rather than by guesswork, and about the cases where the method's own guarantees run out.

Takeaway: We need a rule that replaces "pick the right root" with "pick the right subspace", plus a numerically honest way to compute that subspace. The next section looks for the linear problem that the quadratic equation is hiding.

2. Where is the linear problem hiding?

Question: The Riccati equation is quadratic in \(X\), so where is the linear problem inside it that would let us choose a plane instead of guessing a root?

Look at the optimality conditions instead of at the equation. Let \(\lambda_k\) be the costate, the gradient of the remaining cost with respect to \(x_k\). Stationarity in \(u_k\) gives \(u_k = -R^{-1}B'\lambda_{k+1}\), and substituting it into the dynamics and the costate recursion gives a pair of relations that are linear in \((x,\lambda)\):

\[ x_{k+1} = A x_k - G\lambda_{k+1}, \qquad \lambda_k = Q x_k + A'\lambda_{k+1}, \qquad G = BR^{-1}B' = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}. \]

Collected into one statement that links step \(k\) to step \(k+1\), that is a relation between two matrices, not an eigenvalue problem for one:

The Riccati pencil of the running example
\[ L \begin{pmatrix} x_k \\ \lambda_k \end{pmatrix} = N \begin{pmatrix} x_{k+1} \\ \lambda_{k+1} \end{pmatrix}, \qquad L = \begin{pmatrix} A & 0 \\ -Q & I \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} \tfrac12 & 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 1 & \tfrac12 & 0 & 0 \\ -\tfrac12 & \tfrac14 & 1 & 0 \\ \tfrac14 & -\tfrac12 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \quad N = \begin{pmatrix} I & G \\ 0 & A' \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & \tfrac12 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 & \tfrac12 \end{pmatrix}. \]

Both are \(4\times 4\) here, and both are invertible for this plant: \(\det L = \det N = \det A = -\tfrac34\). A plane on which \(\lambda = Xx\) and which the dynamics map into itself is what the word "solution" should mean, and that condition has a one-line form:

\[ L\begin{pmatrix} I \\ X \end{pmatrix} = N\begin{pmatrix} I \\ X \end{pmatrix} T \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad A = (I+GX)T \;\;\text{and}\;\; X = Q + A'XT. \]

Eliminating \(T\) between those two block rows returns the Riccati equation, with \(T = A - BK\). On the running example this elimination can be checked in rationals at \(X = P = I\): \(T\) comes out \(\begin{pmatrix}0&\tfrac12\\ \tfrac12&0\end{pmatrix}\), which is \(A - BK\) exactly, and \(X - Q - A'XT\) is the zero matrix. So the quadratic equation and the pencil carry the same information; the pencil expresses it as geometry.

Where do the candidate planes live? In the pencil's spectrum. Its multipliers \(z\) are the roots of \(\det(L - zN)\), and for this pencil

\[ \det(L - zN) = -\tfrac34 + \tfrac{51}{16}z^2 - \tfrac34 z^4 = -\tfrac{3}{16}\,(4z^2 - 1)(z^2 - 4), \]

so the four multipliers are \(2, -2, \tfrac12, -\tfrac12\), and they come in reciprocal pairs, \(z \leftrightarrow 1/z\): \(2 \leftrightarrow \tfrac12\) and \(-2 \leftrightarrow -\tfrac12\). That pairing is the Riccati structure showing through the spectrum, and it is the reason a pencil of this kind never happens to be all-stable: every multiplier inside the disk drags one outside.

Now the selection problem, exactly. A solution of the DARE is a choice of \(n = 2\) of those four multipliers whose deflating subspace is a graph. There are \(\binom{4}{2} = 6\) choices, and all six can be solved in rational arithmetic, because the multipliers are exact:

What the table still does not tell you is how to find one of those planes. The multipliers are the roots of a degree-four polynomial, the six pairs are not ordered by anything obvious, and four of them are dead ends that only reveal themselves after you have built a basis and looked at its top block. So the requirement is now concrete: a procedure that produces a deflating subspace directly, for a selection that can be stated afterwards rather than searched for, using only transformations that do not amplify rounding error.

Takeaway: Riccati solutions are in one-to-one correspondence with the pencil's deflating subspaces that are graphs; the stabilizing one is the plane attached to the multipliers inside the disk. The problem is now linear algebra: compute that plane, then test whether it is a graph.

3. Why not just take two eigenvectors?

Question: Section 2 reduced the problem to one deflating subspace of a \(4\times4\) pencil. For this plant \(N\) is invertible, so why not form \(N^{-1}L\), compute its four eigenvectors, keep the two stable ones and stack them?

Two reasons, and both are measurable. The first is that the matrix product is a derived object that does not always exist. The second is that even when it does, an eigenvector is the wrong unit of accuracy.

(a) The plant may have no invertible \(A\)

Take \(P = I\), \(G\) as before, and the closed loop \(M = \begin{pmatrix} \tfrac14 & \tfrac14 \\ \tfrac14 & \tfrac14 \end{pmatrix}\), which designs the plant

\[ A = \begin{pmatrix} \tfrac34 & \tfrac34 \\ \tfrac34 & \tfrac34 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad Q = \begin{pmatrix} \tfrac58 & -\tfrac38 \\ -\tfrac38 & \tfrac58 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \det A = 0,\ \det Q = \tfrac14. \]

The cost weight is positive definite and the Riccati answer is perfectly ordinary. But \(\det N = \det A = 0\), so \(N^{-1}L\) does not exist: the matrix formulation has nothing to say. The pencil still does, and it says something specific. Its characteristic polynomial comes out

\[ \det(L - zN) = -\tfrac32 z + \tfrac{15}{4}z^2 - \tfrac32 z^3, \]

a cubic where a quartic was owed. The missing degree is the pencil announcing a multiplier at infinity; the finite multipliers are \(\tfrac12\), \(2\), \(0\). And the answer is still there: in exact arithmetic the pair \(\{0, \tfrac12\}\) gives \(\|X - P\| = 0\), a DARE residual of \(0\), and a deflating identity \(\max\|LV - NVT\| = 0\). Lancaster and Rodman make precisely this point when they note that the pencil formulation drops the invertibility assumption on \(A\), and that when \(A\) is singular the pencil has a multiplier at infinity, "a phenomenon which cannot arise in a classical eigenvalue problem".

Disclosed honestly: the double-precision kernel that produced every other number on this page has no infinite-multiplier deflation, so on this pencil it refuses (a sweep detects a negligible \(\beta\) and declines to divide by it). The exact-arithmetic result above is a statement about the pencil, not about our sweeps. Production QZ codes do implement that deflation.

(b) Eigenvectors are the wrong unit of accuracy

Now keep the plant invertible and make the eigenvectors bad. Fix the two stable multipliers at \(\tfrac12\) and \(\tfrac{49}{100}\) and let the closed loop be \(M = \begin{pmatrix} \tfrac12 & c \\ 0 & \tfrac{49}{100} \end{pmatrix}\) with the same \(G = \begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix}\). Because \(M\) stays lower triangular, the pencil's four multipliers are the same for every \(c\), and so is the answer: \(X = I\) throughout. Only the directions change. That is the honest accounting. What the pencil formulation buys is not immunity to the problem's conditioning. It is three absences: no \(N^{-1}L\) to form (so (a) cannot happen), no eigenvector matrix of the whole pencil whose condition is set by directions you never use (\(\kappa(V_4)\) reaches \(3.5\times10^3\) while the two vectors you need are only \(10^4\) apart from parallel), and no division by a nearly vanishing quantity on the way, because the subspace is carried as the range of an orthogonal matrix rather than as a list of vectors.

Intuition: A deflating subspace is an object. A list of eigenvectors is one coordinate system on that object, and coordinate systems can be badly conditioned or fail to exist while the object is in perfect shape. Compute the object.

So the specification for the algorithm is now fixed by the two failures above:

  1. Work with the pair \((L,N)\), never the product. Nothing may require \(N\) or a subblock of \(N\) to be invertible.
  2. Carry the subspace, not vectors. The basis must stay orthonormal under rounding, because a basis of nearly parallel vectors is worth nothing however accurately it is stored.
  3. Decide which subspace last. The selection among the multipliers should be a reordering of something already computed, not a search over the \(\binom{2n}{n}\) candidates of section 2.
Takeaway: Eigenvectors one at a time need \(N^{-1}L\) to exist and a well-conditioned basis for the whole pencil; both can fail while the answer is fine. What we need is an orthogonal equivalence of the pencil that makes a chosen set of multipliers separable as a leading block.

4. What the generalized Schur form gives you

Question: What does an algorithm build when the output has to be a subspace, the choice has to be made afterwards, and nothing may be inverted? And why does that object contain the Riccati solution?

The answer is the generalized real Schur form of the pencil, computed by the QZ algorithm: two orthogonal matrices \(U, V\) of the same size as the pencil such that

The decomposition (LAPACK's DGGES computes this)
\[ U'LV = S = \begin{pmatrix} * & * & * & * \\ 0 & * & * & * \\ 0 & 0 & * & * \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & * \end{pmatrix}, \qquad U'NV = R = \begin{pmatrix} * & * & * & * \\ 0 & * & * & * \\ 0 & 0 & * & * \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & * \end{pmatrix}, \]

with \(S\) quasi-upper-triangular (diagonal blocks of size 1 or 2, so that a real arithmetic form can hold a conjugate pair) and \(R\) upper triangular. The multipliers are read blockwise as \(z = \alpha/\beta\) from the diagonal pairs, in homogeneous form: a multiplier at infinity is \(\beta = 0\), which is representable here and not representable in any list of eigenvalues of \(N^{-1}L\). Set against section 3's three requirements, the form is exactly the thing: \(U\) and \(V\) are orthogonal, no inverse of \(N\) appears anywhere, and the choice of subspace is a reordering of the diagonal blocks.

The property that makes it a Riccati tool is deflation. Cut both triangular matrices after the first \(k\) rows and columns, and the leading \(k\) columns of \(V\), say \(V_k\), satisfy

\[ L V_k = N V_k T_k, \qquad T_k = R_{11}^{-1}S_{11}, \]

so \(\operatorname{range}(V_k)\) is a \(k\)-dimensional deflating subspace of the pencil with internal dynamics \(T_k\). Only the small \(k\times k\) block \(R_{11}\) is inverted, never \(N\). Van Dooren's theorem on regular pencils is the other half of the licence: the spectrum of a regular pencil can be partitioned arbitrarily between the two triangular blocks, so any choice of multipliers can be made the leading deflating subspace by reordering. Selecting a Riccati solution is therefore a reordering problem, not a search over the \(\binom{2n}{n}\) candidates of section 2.

What each stage has to be careful about

  1. Shape, then cost. The Hessenberg-triangular reduction costs \(O((2n)^3)\) once and makes every later sweep \(O((2n)^2)\) instead of \(O((2n)^3)\). It is the same trade the ordinary QR iteration makes, and here it is \(875\) multiply-add units against \(3664\) for six whole sweeps.
  2. A sweep can be silent. A \(2\times2\) diagonal block holding two real multipliers does not move under a double-shift sweep: the sweep's shift polynomial is that block's own characteristic polynomial, so \(p(M)e_1 = 0\) by Cayley-Hamilton. One rotation pair finishes it instead. The kernel used here rehearses those rotations on a \(2\times2\) copy and refuses the split if it would leave a subdiagonal above \(10^{-11}\) of the block scale; section 6 shows what that refusal buys.
  3. Reordering is the delicate step. Each adjacent swap solves a small Sylvester-type problem whose conditioning is the chordal distance between the two multipliers being exchanged, and those gaps here are \(0.6\) to \(1.0\): comfortable. LAPACK's dtgexc.f answers INFO = 1 with exactly the words "would be too far from generalized Schur form; the problem is ill conditioned" when a swap cannot be made safely, which is the same judgement the rehearsal above makes.

Now the payoff, stated with the assumptions it actually needs.

Claim: Let \((S,R) = (U'LV, U'NV)\) be a generalized real Schur form of the Riccati pencil with \(V\) partitioned as \(\begin{pmatrix} V_{11} & V_{12} \\ V_{21} & V_{22}\end{pmatrix}\) by \(n\). Suppose the diagonal blocks are ordered so that the leading \(n\) carry the chosen multipliers, and suppose \(R_{11}\) and \(V_{11}\) are invertible. Then \(X = V_{21}V_{11}^{-1}\) solves the DARE, and the closed loop is \(A - BK = V_{11} T V_{11}^{-1}\) with \(T = R_{11}^{-1}S_{11}\). So the multipliers in the leading block are the closed-loop multipliers: choosing them inside the disk is choosing a stabilizing gain.

Proof sketch. Because \(S\) and \(R\) are block upper triangular with the cut after \(n\), the leading \(n\) columns \(V_1 = \begin{pmatrix}V_{11}\\ V_{21}\end{pmatrix}\) of \(V\) obey \(LV_1 = NV_1T\) with \(T = R_{11}^{-1}S_{11}\) (\(R_{11}\) invertible is used exactly here). Write \(X = V_{21}V_{11}^{-1}\) and multiply out the two block rows of \(LV_1 = NV_1T\) on the right by \(V_{11}^{-1}\): the first row gives \(A = (I + GX)T\), the second gives \(X = Q + A'XT\). Eliminating \(T\) yields \(X = Q + A'X(I+GX)^{-1}A\), which is the DARE written with \(G = BR^{-1}B'\); the push-through identity \(X(I+GX)^{-1} = X - XB(R + B'XB)^{-1}B'X\) turns it back into the form in section 1. Each of those steps was checked in rationals on the running example, including \((I+GX)^{-1}A = A - BK\) exactly. \(V_{11}\) invertible is the content of section 2's table: it is what "is a graph" means. The claim is about a deflating subspace, so it does not need \(A\), \(N\), or any eigenvector to be invertible or well conditioned.

On the running pencil the two tests in the hypothesis are measured, not assumed: \(|\det R_{11}| = 1.875\) and \(|\det V_{11}| = 0.500\), and the extracted answer is \(X = V_{21}V_{11}^{-1} = I\) to ten decimal places.

One trap worth naming, because it is easy to fall into when reading \(T\) off a QZ output. Here \(T = R_{11}^{-1}S_{11} = \operatorname{diag}(-\tfrac12, \tfrac12)\), while the closed loop this example was designed from is \(M = \begin{pmatrix}0&\tfrac12\\\tfrac12&0\end{pmatrix}\). Their entrywise difference has norm \(5.0\times10^{-1}\), which looks like a failure and is not one: \(T\) is the same operator expressed in the QZ basis, \(M\) in the graph basis. Change basis and \(\|V_{11}TV_{11}^{-1} - M\| = 6.9\times10^{-15}\), and the graph identity itself closes: \(\|A - (I+GX)V_{11}TV_{11}^{-1}\| = 7.2\times10^{-15}\). The subspace and the operator are the invariants; the matrices you write down are coordinates.

Takeaway: The generalized Schur form turns the pencil into a triangular pair whose diagonal blocks are the candidate Riccati planes. Reorder so that the multipliers you want are on top, check \(R_{11}\) and \(V_{11}\), and \(X\) is a \(n\times n\) solve away.

5. The procedure, the answer, and what it costs

Question: What are the steps, what do they give on the running example, and what do they cost next to just iterating the Riccati recursion?
  1. Build the pencil. Form \(L = \begin{pmatrix}A&0\\-Q&I\end{pmatrix}\) and \(N = \begin{pmatrix}I&G\\0&A'\end{pmatrix}\) from \(A\), \(Q\) and \(G = BR^{-1}B'\). Size \(2n \times 2n\). Do not invert anything.
  2. Reduce to Hessenberg-triangular form by orthogonal row and column operations: \(U'LV = H\), \(U'NV = T\), with \(T\) triangular throughout.
  3. Sweep to the generalized Schur form. Francis double-shift sweeps on the pair, deflating a subdiagonal when it becomes negligible relative to its own neighbourhood, and splitting a \(2\times2\) block that holds two real multipliers (the rehearsal described in section 4). Stop when every diagonal block is settled.
  4. Order the blocks. Bubble adjacent blocks until the \(n\) multipliers you want, here the ones with \(|z| \lt 1\), occupy the leading block. This is the step that replaces the \(\binom{2n}{n}\) search of section 2.
  5. Test the two hypotheses. \(\sigma_{\min}(R_{11})\) not negligible (equivalently: no chosen multiplier at infinity) and \(|\det V_{11}|\) not negligible (the plane is a graph). If either fails, the selection is not a solution, and the honest output is "no \(X\)" rather than a large matrix.
  6. Extract, then check. \(X = V_{21}V_{11}^{-1}\), \(K = (R + B'XB)^{-1}B'XA\), and then verify two independent things: the DARE residual, and the closed-loop moduli of \(A - BK\). Section 6 shows a case where the first is tiny and the second is the only warning.

Iterating the recursion is not wrong here (the plant is detectable, and \(A\) being unstable is exactly the case you need a solver for, not a case that breaks the iteration); it is just a different accuracy-per-unit-of-work curve. Quadratic convergence is what the structure-preserving doubling family buys instead, and it is the route worth knowing once \(2n \times 2n\) stops being small; §7 names it.

Takeaway: Build the pencil, reduce it by orthogonal equivalences, reorder, test \(R_{11}\) and \(V_{11}\), extract, and check the residual and the closed-loop moduli. On a plant whose modes are coupled in the cost this is \(O((2n)^3)\) once plus \(O((2n)^2)\) per sweep, and it delivers the stabilizing solution to working precision.

6. Where the guarantee stops

Question: Section 4's Claim needs three things the running example hands out for free: multipliers that can be told apart, a chosen plane that is not vertical, and a unit disk whose inside is decidable. Change one input at a time. Which change breaks the answer, which one only looks dangerous, and which quantity tells you the difference?

Each case below changes exactly one input of a pencil and keeps everything else, including the answer the pencil was designed from. It is worth fixing the predictions before reading the tables: two nearly equal multipliers should be the frightening case, a zero weight should be boring (zero cost is still a well-posed problem), and the DARE residual should be the last thing to complain.

(a) One weight goes to zero: \(Q = \operatorname{diag}(0, q_2)\)

Take the discretized double integrator \(A = \begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix}\), \(B = \begin{pmatrix}0\\1\end{pmatrix}\), \(R = [\,1\,]\) and \(Q = \begin{pmatrix}0&0\\0&q_2\end{pmatrix}\): the cost penalizes the velocity and never looks at the position. \(A\)'s multiplier is \(1\) twice over and its only eigenvector is \((1,0)\), the pure-position direction, and \(Q^{1/2}\) kills that vector for every \(q_2\). So \((A, Q^{1/2})\) is undetectable at \(q_2 = \tfrac{1}{10}\) as much as at \(q_2 = 0\). This family has a closed form, which makes it a fair test of what detectability was buying.

The pencil still factorizes; the word "stabilizing" is what loses its content.

(d) The shared eigenvector: a pencil that refuses, and an answer that passes the test

Change one entry of the last family: closed loop \(M = \begin{pmatrix}\tfrac12&\eta\\0&\tfrac12\end{pmatrix}\), again designed from \(P = I\) with the same \(G\). In rationals the pencil's multipliers are \(\tfrac12, \tfrac12, 2, 2\) for every \(\eta\), so nothing in the spectrum distinguishes \(\eta = 0\) from \(\eta = 1\). The kernel of \(L - zN\) does: at \(\eta = 0\) it is two-dimensional at both \(z = \tfrac12\) and \(z = 2\), and at \(\eta = 1\) it is one-dimensional at both. Algebraic multiplicity two, geometric multiplicity one: the pencil is defective. The same collapse of the kernel is measured at \(\eta = 10^{-3}\) and at \(\eta = 100\), so every nonzero \(\eta\) in the table below is defective, at every scale.

The gap column measures the tightest chordal distance between multipliers the finished form leaves in separate blocks, so a row reading \(2.7\times10^{-16}\) is one where the form could not pull two blocks apart, and the \(\eta = 0\) row's \(2.2\times10^{-16}\) is the distance between two blocks holding the same multiplier, which never have to pass each other.

Only the \(\eta = 0\) row reorders. Every other row ends with ok = False and one or two \(2\times2\) blocks kept whole, which is the same judgement LAPACK's dtgexc.f renders as INFO = 1: the requested exchange "would be too far from generalized Schur form; the problem is ill conditioned".

The row to stare at is \(\eta = 1\). The reordering refuses, the extraction runs anyway on whatever the un-reordered form happens to leave in the leading block, and the matrix it hands back solves the DARE to \(8.7\times10^{-15}\). That is not noise and not garbage. The leading block ended up holding the pair \(\{2, 2\}\), and by the Claim of section 4 the plane over those two multipliers is a correct solution of the right equation with the wrong closed loop: the second kind of answer that section 2 tabulated for the running plant, reached here by a route that never got to choose. \(\|X - P\| = 5.766\), the closed loop is \(2.000, 2.000\), and the residual says nothing about either.

\(\eta = 10^{-6}\) fails in the other direction: the block refuses to split, \(V_{11}\) comes out nearly singular, and error and residual both blow up together (\(1.2\times10^{12}\) and \(2.1\times10^{11}\)), with a closed loop at \(1.650, 1.989\) that belongs to no intended design. Between them, \(\eta = 10^{-3}\) and \(10^{-2}\) show the intermediate regime, a refused swap and an \(X\) whose residual is a modest \(1.7\times10^{-2}\) or a tidy \(2.2\times10^{-8}\) while its error is \(10^{4}\) to \(10^{6}\).

What changes: not the multipliers, not the equation, only how many eigenvectors each multiplier keeps. Why: the deflating subspace for \(\{\tfrac12, \tfrac12\}\) still exists, but moving it to the leading block means solving a Sylvester-type exchange equation between adjacent blocks whose conditioning is the chordal distance between the multipliers being swapped. When the two blocks are about to hold the same multiplier and only one eigenvector is available between them, that exchange problem has no stable answer. The subspace exists; the ordered form does not. A defective pencil has deflating subspaces that no sequence of orthogonal block swaps can express separately.

(e) What the route does not preserve

On the running example, the extracted \(X\) differs from its own transpose by \(3.2\times10^{-15}\) while it differs from the exact answer by \(3.3\times10^{-15}\). Symmetry is an accident here, not a guarantee: nothing in \(X = V_{21}V_{11}^{-1}\) forces \(V_{21}V_{11}^{-1} = (V_{21}V_{11}^{-1})'\), and the standard repair is to average, \(\tfrac12(X + X')\), before forming the gain. Under the ill-conditioning of section 3(b) the asymmetry is still small in absolute terms but no longer negligible next to the answer: at \(c = 100\), \(\|X - X'\| = 2.13\times10^{-12}\) against \(\|X - P\| = 6.64\times10^{-10}\) and a residual of \(2.15\times10^{-8}\). The route that respects the structure is not to reorder a general pencil but to do arithmetic in the pencil's own symmetry class, which is what the structure-preserving doubling family and Byers and Benner's pencil arithmetic are for; the aside at the end of section 5 is the same point.

Where the guarantee ends, and what to check

Section 4's Claim is conditional and none of its conditions is free. Detectability is not a hypothesis of the algorithm, it is a hypothesis of the answer: (a) returns a solution with a \(10^{-16}\) residual in every row while its closed loop keeps a mode on the circle. The disk is a decision, not a computation: (b) shows the count of multipliers inside it flipping between 0, 1 and 2 under a diagonal perturbation of \(10^{-13}\) that displaces a multiplier by \(1.5\times10^{-8}\). Reordering is a numerical problem with its own conditioning: (d) shows one pencil, with one unchanged spectrum, refusing to be put in the order the selection needs, and then handing back a correct solution of the wrong kind. And (c) shows the case everyone fears, two multipliers \(6\times10^{-16}\) apart, passing without incident.

There is a fourth limit, of scale rather than of structure. The same kernel, run on a \(48\times48\) pencil (\(n = 24\)) from the family of section 5, spent all \(960\) allowed sweeps, left \(24\) blocks of size \(2\), never reordered at all (\(0\) swaps, ok = False), and returned \(\|X - P\| = 7.2\times10^{15}\) after \(54\) seconds. The tightest gap it was asked to cross was \(4.2\times10^{-4}\), so the pencil was not objecting; the implementation was, because it has no aggressive early deflation and one stubborn interior block costs the whole pencil a sweep. Production dgges plus dtgexc is the answer at that size, and beyond a few thousand states the dense \(2n \times 2n\) pencil is itself the objection.

Ordered by how much each signal deserves to be trusted:

  1. The reordering's own refusal. \(2\times2\) blocks left holding a mixed pair, or a swap that was declined, is the only signal that fired correctly in every failure above.
  2. \(\sigma_{\min}(R_{11})\) and \(\sigma_{\min}(V_{11})\). The first says no chosen multiplier is at infinity, the second says the plane is a graph. In section 5's float table these were \(10^{0}\) and \(10^{-16}\) respectively on exactly the rows that had to fail.
  3. The closed-loop moduli of \(A - BK\). Cheap, independent of the pencil, and the only number that connects the output to the requirement.
  4. The DARE residual. Last, and not as a certificate: the \(\eta = 1\) row of (d) reports \(8.7\times10^{-15}\) on an answer \(5.8\) away from the one wanted.
Takeaway: The generalized Schur route fails out loud when it fails at all, provided you read the right signal. It cannot decide which side of the unit circle a multiplier is on when the pencil sits on that circle, and it cannot separate a defective pencil into the block it needs; both show up in the ordering flag and in \(\sigma_{\min}(V_{11})\). What it cannot tell you by itself is whether the answer is the LQR answer. That is detectability's job, and the residual is the weakest of the four checks.
