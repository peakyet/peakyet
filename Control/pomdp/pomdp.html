POMDPs: how to decide when you cannot see the state

A partially observable Markov decision process turns uncertainty itself into the state of the planning problem. Instead of asking “where am I?”, the controller maintains a probability distribution over where it might be, predicts how actions move that distribution, corrects it with observations, and chooses actions for both their immediate effect and the information they create.

1. The problem POMDPs solve

Why add machinery beyond an ordinary Markov decision process (MDP)? Because real controllers rarely observe the physical state directly. A robot sees noisy pixels, a diagnostic system sees imperfect test results, and a network controller sees delayed telemetry. Acting as though the latest observation is the state throws away history; remembering the complete history makes planning grow without bound.

The compression trick is the belief state \(b_t\), where \(b_t(s)=\Pr(s_t=s\mid h_t)\) and \(h_t\) is everything observed and done so far. Under the POMDP assumptions, this probability distribution contains every part of history that matters for predicting the future. The process is therefore Markov in belief space even when it is not Markov in raw observations.

Mental picture: an MDP controller occupies one physical state. A POMDP controller occupies one point in a probability simplex; moving through that simplex is inference plus control.
Takeaway: belief is a bounded, decision-relevant replacement for an ever-growing action-observation history.

2. The model, with only the parts that do work

Why name the components? Because solving a POMDP means repeatedly composing three mechanisms: physical dynamics, sensing, and utility. A finite discounted POMDP is commonly written

\[ (\mathcal S,\mathcal A,\mathcal O,T,Z,R,\gamma,b_0). \]
symbolengineering meaningquestion it answers
\(s\in\mathcal S\)hidden physical stateWhat is actually true?
\(a\in\mathcal A\)actionWhat can the controller do?

The timing convention above is: choose \(a_t\), transition from \(s_t\) to \(s_{t+1}\), then receive \(o_{t+1}\). Some texts attach observations to the old state; either convention works if the equations and implementation agree.

Modeling warning: a solver cannot rescue a bad state definition. The hidden state must contain enough information that the next state and observation depend on the past only through the current state and action.
Takeaway: the transition model describes the world, the observation model describes the sensor, and reward states the objective.

3. Belief update: predict, then correct

Why derive the filter? Every planning step assumes the controller can convert its current belief, selected action, and new observation into the next belief. This update has the same structure as a Bayesian filter.

Correct with evidencemultiply by \(Z(o_{t+1}\mid s',a_t)\), then normalize
Figure 2. Control changes what states are possible; sensing reweights those possibilities.

First predict the next-state distribution, because the action changes the physical world before the new sensor reading arrives:

\[ \bar b_{t+1}(s')=\sum_{s\in\mathcal S}T(s'\mid s,a_t)b_t(s). \]

Then apply Bayes' rule, because states that better explain the observation should receive more posterior mass:

\[ b_{t+1}(s')=\tau(b_t,a_t,o_{t+1})(s') =\frac{Z(o_{t+1}\mid s',a_t)\,\bar b_{t+1}(s')} {\sum_{x\in\mathcal S}Z(o_{t+1}\mid x,a_t)\,\bar b_{t+1}(x)}. \]

The denominator is not cosmetic: it is both the normalizing constant and the predictive probability of receiving that observation,

\[ \Pr(o\mid b,a)=\sum_{s'}Z(o\mid s',a)\sum_sT(s'\mid s,a)b(s). \]
function updateBelief(b, action, observation):
    predicted[s2] = sum_s T[s2 | s, action] * b[s]
    weighted[s2]  = Z[observation | s2, action] * predicted[s2]
    return weighted / sum(weighted)
Why belief is sufficient: once \(b_t\) is known, integrating over it produces the same distribution over every future trajectory as integrating over the full history. The planner loses no predictive information by discarding that history.
Takeaway: a POMDP step is a controlled state prediction followed by a Bayesian measurement correction.

4. Planning in belief space

Why convert the problem to belief space? It lets ordinary dynamic-programming logic apply. The immediate reward at belief \(b\) is simply the state reward averaged under that belief:

\[ r(b,a)=\sum_s b(s)R(s,a). \]

The optimal value obeys a Bellman equation, but the successor is random because the future observation is random:

\[ V^*(b)=\max_{a\in\mathcal A}\left[r(b,a)+\gamma\sum_{o\in\mathcal O} \Pr(o\mid b,a)V^*(\tau(b,a,o))\right]. \]

Each candidate action therefore earns two kinds of value. It changes the world and earns immediate reward; it also changes which observations will arrive, which changes future beliefs and future decisions. The latter is the value of information. It is why a POMDP may deliberately inspect, probe, listen, or move to a better viewpoint even when that action has a short-term cost.

State-changing action

Its main purpose is to improve the physical state: move, repair, grasp, transmit, open.

\(T(\cdot\mid s,a)\) changes strongly

Information-gathering action

Its main purpose is to separate hypotheses: inspect, scan, test, listen, query.

\(Z(\cdot\mid s',a)\) becomes informative
Takeaway: optimal POMDP actions are chosen for their effect on both the world and the controller's future knowledge.

5. Worked example: listen before opening

Why use the Tiger problem? It is the smallest example in which information has obvious economic value. A tiger is behind the left or right door. Opening the safe door earns \(+10\); opening the tiger door earns \(-100\). Listening costs \(-1\) and reports the tiger's side correctly with probability \(0.85\). Let \(p=\Pr(\text{tiger is left})\), and use \(\gamma=0.95\).

At \(p=0.5\), opening either door has expected reward \(-45\). If the controller hears “left,” Bayes' rule gives

\[ \Pr(L\mid \text{hear }L)= \frac{0.85(0.5)}{0.85(0.5)+0.15(0.5)}=0.85. \]

It should then open the right door, whose expected reward is \(0.85(10)+0.15(-100)=-6.5\). The symmetric result holds after hearing “right,” so listening is worth

\[ Q_{\text{listen}}(0.5)=-1+0.95(-6.5)=-7.175, \]

which is much better than \(-45\). Listening is optimal for approximately \(0.156\le p\le0.844\); outside that interval, the belief is already decisive enough that paying for information is not worthwhile in this two-step horizon.

6. Exact finite-horizon solution: conditional plans and alpha vectors

Why can a continuous belief space sometimes be solved exactly? For a fixed finite horizon, every deterministic conditional plan has a value that is linear in the initial belief. If plan \(\pi\) yields state-conditioned returns \(\alpha_\pi(s)\), then

\[ V_\pi(b)=\sum_s b(s)\alpha_\pi(s)=\alpha_\pi^\top b. \]

The optimal policy chooses the best conditional plan, so its value is the maximum of linear functions:

\[ V_H(b)=\max_{\alpha\in\Gamma_H}\alpha^\top b. \]
\(\alpha_1^\top b\)
\(\alpha_2^\top b\)
\(\alpha_3^\top b\)
upper envelope \(V_H(b)\)
Figure 4. Maximizing over conditional plans creates a piecewise-linear, convex value function. A line that never reaches the upper envelope is dominated and can be removed.

Why does the backup branch over observations? After selecting the current action, a conditional plan may choose a different continuation vector \(\alpha_o\) for each possible observation \(o\). One candidate backed-up vector is

\[ \alpha(s)=R(s,a)+\gamma\sum_{s'}T(s'\mid s,a) \sum_o Z(o\mid s',a)\alpha_o(s'). \]

Exact value iteration enumerates these action-and-continuation combinations, then prunes vectors that are never optimal at any belief. Incremental pruning and witness-style methods avoid retaining the full combinatorial cross-product longer than necessary.

Gamma = {zero-vector}
repeat for each horizon step:
    candidates = {}
    for action a:
        for each choice of one continuation alpha_o per observation o:
            candidates.add(back_up(a, {alpha_o}))
    Gamma = remove_vectors_that_never_win(candidates)
return Gamma
Why exact methods become difficult: beliefs live in a simplex of dimension \(|\mathcal S|-1\), while each action can branch into \(|\mathcal O|\) continuation plans. The number of useful policy trees can still grow explosively with horizon.
Takeaway: exact finite-horizon planning is dynamic programming over conditional plans, compressed into the upper envelope of alpha vectors.

7. How POMDPs are solved in practice

Why are there several solver families? The belief-MDP reduction is exact but continuous, so practical methods differ mainly in which beliefs and futures they choose to represent.

small, discrete, offline

Exact alpha-vector methods

Use finite-horizon value iteration, witness methods, or incremental pruning when state, action, observation, and horizon sizes are genuinely small.

Output: a policy valid over the full belief simplex, with exact finite-horizon values.

moderate, discrete, offline

Point-based methods

PBVI, HSVI, and SARSOP back up selected reachable beliefs instead of the whole simplex. This spends computation where the policy is likely to visit.

Output: an approximate reusable policy, often with bounds or error guarantees under method-specific assumptions.

large, generative model, online

Monte Carlo tree search

POMCP samples hidden states and futures from a simulator, building an action-observation search tree only around the current history.

Output: the next action after a computation budget, followed by replanning after the real observation.

A useful selection rule is based less on algorithm fashion than on the interface your system can supply:

Approximation target: good solvers rarely approximate “every possible belief equally.” They concentrate on beliefs reachable from \(b_0\), especially those reachable under strong policies.
Takeaway: choose an exact, point-based, or simulation-based solver according to model access, reachable-belief complexity, and when the action must be produced.

8. An engineering workflow that usually works

Why finish with a workflow? Most POMDP failures are modeling or validation failures, not failures to memorize another solver acronym.

  1. Define the hidden state minimally but sufficiently. Include every latent variable needed to make dynamics and sensing conditionally Markov; omit history that belief filtering already summarizes.
  2. Write a generative step first. Given \((s,a)\), sample \((s',o,r)\). Even when using matrices later, this interface enables simulation tests and online solvers.
  3. Validate probabilities and timing. Check that every transition and observation distribution sums to one and that the code agrees on whether observations depend on \(s\) or \(s'\).
  4. Test the belief filter independently. Use deterministic edge cases, impossible observations, symmetric priors, and Monte Carlo frequency checks.
  5. Build a tiny exact instance. Shrink the state space or horizon until exact enumeration is possible; use it as an oracle for the approximate pipeline.
  6. Choose the solver from the bottleneck. Alpha vectors address small exact models, point-based methods address large belief spaces, and online sampling addresses large generative models.
  7. Evaluate closed-loop behavior. Compare expected return, tail risk, belief calibration, runtime per action, and sensitivity to model mismatch across many simulated episodes.
b = initialBelief
while not terminal:
    a = planner.chooseAction(b)       # offline policy lookup or online search
    execute(a)
    o, r = receiveObservationReward()
    b = updateBelief(b, a, o)
    planner.reuseSearchTreeIfPossible(a, o)
Common implementation bug: selecting an action from observation \(o_t\) alone produces a reactive policy, not a general POMDP policy. Two identical current observations can demand different actions when their histories imply different beliefs.
Takeaway: model, filter, solve, and validate as separate modules; use a tiny exact problem to keep the full stack honest.

9. Where POMDPs fit

Why connect the theory? A POMDP is easiest to remember as the intersection of three familiar ideas:

Hidden Markov model

Supplies latent-state dynamics and Bayesian filtering. A POMDP adds actions and rewards.

Markov decision process

Supplies Bellman recursion and policy optimization. A POMDP replaces observed states with beliefs.

Bayesian decision theory

Supplies expected utility under uncertainty. A POMDP makes decisions sequential and lets actions affect future evidence.

Partially observable RL

Handles unknown models or learned policies. Recurrent state is often an implicit approximation to a belief state.

Takeaway: a POMDP is Bayesian filtering coupled to sequential decision-making, with information acquisition inside the control loop.

10. The whole mechanism in five lines

  1. The true state \(s_t\) is hidden, so maintain a belief \(b_t(s)\).
  2. After action \(a_t\), predict through \(T\), then correct with observation likelihood \(Z\).
  3. Treat beliefs as states and apply the Bellman principle.
  4. Include how actions change future information, not only immediate reward.
  5. Solve exactly with alpha vectors when small; otherwise focus computation on reachable beliefs or sampled futures.
Final takeaway: POMDPs are not “MDPs with noisy inputs”; they are control problems whose state is the controller's posterior uncertainty.
