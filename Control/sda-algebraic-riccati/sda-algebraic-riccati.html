The Structure-Preserving Doubling Algorithm

Solving the algebraic Riccati equation by never solving it. The SDA replaces the Riccati equation over and over by an equivalent one — same stabilizing solution — whose data marches toward zero doubly exponentially. After about ten rewrites the answer is simply sitting there as the constant term. This page builds the algorithm from scratch: two tools you asked for (Schur complements, repeated squaring), the baby case where the trick is obvious, the discrete-time algorithm, the Cayley bridge to continuous time, and the numerics of why “structure-preserving” is the point. Every number on the page was produced by running the algorithm; the demo script is linked at the end.

1 · The problem: an equation you should not solve

You know the equation. For the LQR problem with dynamics \(\dot x = Ax + Bu\) and cost \(\int (x^{\mathsf T}Qx + u^{\mathsf T}Ru)\,dt\), the optimal gain \(K = R^{-1}B^{\mathsf T}X\) hides inside the continuous-time algebraic Riccati equation (CARE):

\[ A^{\mathsf T}X + XA - XGX + Q = 0, \qquad G = BR^{-1}B^{\mathsf T}. \]

It is quadratic in the unknown \(X\), and the solution you want — the stabilizing one, with \(A - GX\) Hurwitz — is one of several. The classical move is to linearize: build the \(2n\times 2n\) Hamiltonian matrix, compute an ordered Schur form, read \(X\) off the stable invariant subspace. That works, but it costs a dense \(2n\)-dimensional eigenproblem, it cannot be warm-started when you re-solve with slightly different weights, and for the large sparse \(n\) that comes from discretizing PDEs it is simply out of reach.

SDA takes a different stance: never solve the equation — keep rewriting it. Each step replaces the equation by an equivalent one (same stabilizing solution \(X\)) whose data is closer to trivial. Here is the discrete-time version of the entire algorithm, all of it:

def sda(A, G, Q, tol=1e-13, kmax=60):
    """Solve X = Q + Aᵀ X (I + GX)⁻¹ A  (the DARE) for its stabilizing solution."""
    I = np.eye(A.shape[0])
    for _ in range(kmax):
        T = np.linalg.inv(I + G @ Q)   # the only inverse; its spectrum lies in [1, ∞)
        A, G, Q = A @ T @ A, G + A @ T @ G @ A.T, Q + A.T @ Q @ T @ A
        if np.linalg.norm(A, 2) < tol: # A_k → 0 doubly exponentially
            break
    return Q                           # Q_k → X, the stabilizing solution

Three lines of updates, one \(n\times n\) inverse per step, and — because the error is squared at every step — machine precision in roughly ten steps. On a random \(n=4\) CARE, reduced to this form, the correct digits of the iterate \(Q_k\) are:

step \(k\)0123456
correct digits of \(Q_k\)−1.4−1.1−0.10.6 3.910.512.7

The final iterate satisfies the original CARE to \(4.7\times10^{-13}\). The number of digits roughly doubles per step — that is the fingerprint of repeated squaring, and it is where the name “doubling” comes from. The rest of the page explains why these three lines do what they claim, and how the continuous-time equation gets reduced to them.

Takeaway: SDA solves the Riccati equation by iterating a rewrite that preserves the solution and squares the error — the algorithm is repeated squaring wearing problem-specific bookkeeping.

2 · Tool 1: Schur complements — elimination for block matrices

Everything below is block Gaussian elimination in disguise, so let’s fix the one identity we need. Take a \(2\times2\)-block matrix \(\begin{bmatrix}P&U\\V&S\end{bmatrix}\) with \(P\) invertible. Gaussian elimination eliminates the \(V\) block: multiply the first block-row by \(VP^{-1}\) and subtract it from the second:

\[ \begin{bmatrix}P&U\\V&S\end{bmatrix} = \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}I&0\\VP^{-1}&I\end{bmatrix}}_{\text{elimination}} \begin{bmatrix}P&0\\0&\chi\end{bmatrix} \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}I&P^{-1}U\\0&I\end{bmatrix}}_{\text{bookkeeping}}, \qquad \chi = S - VP^{-1}U. \]

The little matrix \(\chi = S - VP^{-1}U\) is the Schur complement. Two consequences we will use constantly:

  • It is the price of eliminating a block: to invert or triangularize the big matrix you only ever invert one diagonal block (\(P\), then \(\chi\)).
  • Solving \(\begin{bmatrix}P&U\\V&S\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}x\\y\end{bmatrix} =\begin{bmatrix}f\\g\end{bmatrix}\) reduces to \(\chi y = g - VP^{-1}f\), then \(x = P^{-1}(f - Uy)\).

Quick sanity check with plain scalars: \(\begin{bmatrix}2&1\\1&3\end{bmatrix}\) has \(\chi = 3 - \tfrac{1}{2}\cdot 1 = 2.5\) and indeed \(\det = 2 \times 2.5 = 5\). Whenever you see \((I + G_kQ_k)^{-1}\) appear in SDA below, read: a block got eliminated. That inverse is a Schur complement of a \(2\times2\)-block matrix — and so is every update formula in the algorithm.

Takeaway: a Schur complement is what block Gaussian elimination leaves behind — one small inverse buys the elimination of one block, and SDA is a machine that performs exactly one such elimination per step.

3 · Tool 2: repeated squaring and what “quadratic convergence” means

You want \(F_{1048576}\). The naive way is a chain: multiply by \(\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\1&0\end{bmatrix}\) a million times. The smarter way notices that \(M^{2^k} = \big(M^{2^{k-1}}\big)^2\): each squaring doubles the exponent.

Figure 1. Squaring doubles the exponent, so the distance to the target shrinks geometrically in the exponent. Verified: squaring \(\left[\begin{smallmatrix}1&1\\1&0\end{smallmatrix}\right]\) five times already gives \(F_{32} = 2{,}178{,}309\); twenty squarings give \(F_{1048576}\) versus a million naive products.

Now attach numbers to “fast”. For an error sequence \(e_k\to0\):

  • Linear convergence (a bad name — it means a fixed ratio): \(\|e_{k+1}\| \le c\,\|e_k\|\) with \(0 \lt c \lt 1\). Each step gains \(-\log_{10} c\) correct digits. Slow if \(c\approx1\).
  • Quadratic convergence: \(\|e_{k+1}\| \le C\,\|e_k\|^2\). Each step squares the error, so the number of correct digits roughly doubles. An error of \(10^{-2}\) becomes \(10^{-4}\), then \(10^{-8}\), then \(10^{-16}\): four steps from 2 digits to a full float.

Repeated squaring produces exactly this: if the error after one step is \(\rho\) (with \(\rho \lt 1\)), then after \(k\) squarings it is \(\rho^{2^k}\) — the digits-per-step gain \(-\log_{10}\rho^{2^{k+1}} + \log_{10}\rho^{2^{k}} = 2^{k}(-\log_{10}\rho)\) doubles each step. Try the race yourself:

■ linear: digits = j·(−log₁₀ρ)    ■ doubling: digits = 2k·(−log₁₀ρ)
Demo 1. Correct digits per iteration for a linearly convergent iteration (error \(\rho^j\), gold) versus a doubling iteration (error \(\rho^{2^k}\), green), for a mode ratio \(\rho\). This is an idealized model — one dominant ratio \(\rho\) — but it is exactly the contrast between the plain Riccati fixed-point iteration and SDA later.
Predict before you look: drag \(\rho\) to \(0.9999\) — a nearly-marginal mode, the regime where fixed-point iterations are useless. How many steps does each method need for 16 digits? (The demo’s readout has the answer: about 368,000 steps versus 19. Nearly-singular convergence ratio is precisely where doubling earns its name.)
Takeaway: quadratic convergence means the digit count doubles per step; repeated squaring is the mechanism that produces it, and it is worth any constant factor because it wins by orders of magnitude near \(\rho \approx 1\).

4 · The baby case: doubling for the Stein equation

The doubling trick is easiest to see on a linear matrix equation that shows up everywhere in filtering and control: the Stein equation

\[ X = A^{\mathsf T}XA + Q, \qquad \rho(A) \lt 1,\; Q = Q^{\mathsf T} \succeq 0. \]

Its solution is the infinite series \(X = \sum_{i=0}^{\infty} (A^{\mathsf T})^i Q A^{i}\). The naive “Smith iteration” peels off one term per step: \(X_{j+1} = Q + A^{\mathsf T}X_jA\) — linearly convergent, painful when \(\rho(A)\approx 1\). Instead, substitute the equation into itself:

\[ X = A^{\mathsf T}\big(A^{\mathsf T}XA + Q\big)A + Q = (A^2)^{\mathsf T}XA^2 + \big(A^{\mathsf T}QA + Q\big). \]

Look at what happened: the equation is still a Stein equation, with the same solution \(X\), but the dynamics advanced from \(A\) to \(A^2\) and the constant term absorbed two series terms at once. Substitute again and the dynamics is \(A^4\); after \(k\) substitutions,

\[ X = (A^{2^k})^{\mathsf T}X\,A^{2^k} \;+\; \underbrace{\textstyle\sum_{i=0}^{2^k-1}(A^{\mathsf T})^{i} Q A^{i}}_{Q_k}. \]

Since \(\rho(A) \lt 1\), the first term dies like \(\rho(A)^{2^k}\) — doubly exponentially — so \(Q_k\) converges to \(X\), with digits doubling per rewrite. The rewrite is three lines and never touches an unknown:

\[ A_{k+1} = A_k^2, \qquad Q_{k+1} = Q_k + A_k^{\mathsf T}Q_kA_k, \qquad Q_0 = Q. \]

This is the squared Smith method, and it is the entire SDA pattern in miniature: the equation keeps its form, the solution never moves, the “hard” part \(A_k\) dies, and the answer accumulates in the constant term. Ran on a random \(n=4\) pair with \(\rho(A)=0.93\) (nearly marginal — the hard case):

The digit gains of the doubling row — roughly 0.1, 0.3, 0.5, 1.0, 2.0, 4.0 — double every step, while the plain iteration crawls. Each doubling step literally doubles the number of summed terms.)

Predict: apply the same move to the Riccati equation \(X = Q + A^{\mathsf T}X(I+GX)^{-1}A\) — substitute the equation into itself once. What does the result look like? (Disaster: the inverse’s argument becomes \(I + G\big[Q + A^{\mathsf T}X(I+GX)^{-1}A\big]\) — the equation no longer has the Riccati form, and substituting again makes it worse. The form does not close under naive substitution. Making it close — with the right bookkeeping — is the actual invention of SDA.)
Takeaway: for the linear Stein equation, self-substitution doubles the tail of the series; the equation’s form and solution are invariant while its data dies doubly exponentially.

5 · The real thing: SDA for the discrete Riccati equation

The discrete-time ARE (DARE — LQR for sampled systems, Kalman filters) is

\[ X = Q + A^{\mathsf T}X\,(I + GX)^{-1}A, \qquad G = G^{\mathsf T}\succeq 0, \]

which is the familiar \(X = A^{\mathsf T}XA - A^{\mathsf T}XG(I+XG)^{-1}XA + Q\) after one application of the identity from §2. The plain fixed-point iteration \(X_{j+1} = Q + A^{\mathsf T}X_j(I+GX_j)^{-1}A\), started at \(X_0=0\), converges — but linearly, with ratio \(\rho(E)^2\) where \(E=(I+GX_\star)^{-1}A\) is the closed-loop map. SDA jumps straight to iterate \(2^k\) at step \(k\). Here is why that is possible.

5.1 The scalar picture: fixed-point iteration is a Möbius map, and squaring is free

For scalars, the iteration is a fractional linear (Möbius) map:

\[ x_{j+1} = \frac{q + (qg + a^2)\,x_j}{g\,x_j + 1} \;\longrightarrow\; \text{represented by } M = \begin{bmatrix} qg+a^2 & q \\ g & 1 \end{bmatrix}. \]

Möbius maps compose by multiplying their representing matrices: doing the map twice is \(M^2\), doing it \(2^k\) times is \(M^{2^k}\) — repeated squaring again. And the fixed point is an eigenvector direction of \(M\): iterating from \(x_0=0\) is a power iteration, with error dying like the eigenvalue ratio. So for scalars, “compute the fixed point” and “square the representing matrix” are the same statement.

Intuition: a Riccati fixed-point iteration is the power method for a \(2\times2\) (in truth: \(2n\times2n\)) block matrix whose eigenvector encodes \(X\). Power methods are mediocre; squared power methods are spectacular. SDA is the squared power method, organized so that the squaring never leaves the \((A,G,Q)\) bookkeeping.

5.2 The matrix picture: subspace iteration on a symplectic matrix

For matrices, the representative of the iteration is the block matrix

\[ S \;=\; \begin{bmatrix} I & G \\ 0 & A^{\mathsf T} \end{bmatrix}^{-1} \begin{bmatrix} A & 0 \\ -Q & I \end{bmatrix}. \]

It is symplectic (eigenvalues in reciprocal pairs), and the stabilizing solution is exactly its stable eigenspace. Check by direct multiplication, writing \(Z = \begin{bmatrix}I\\X\end{bmatrix}\):

\[ \begin{bmatrix} A & 0 \\ -Q & I \end{bmatrix}Z = \begin{bmatrix} A \\ X - Q \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \begin{bmatrix} I & G \\ 0 & A^{\mathsf T} \end{bmatrix}Z \; E = \begin{bmatrix} (I+GX)E \\ A^{\mathsf T}X E \end{bmatrix}, \]

and \(S\,Z = Z\,E\) with \(E = (I+GX_\star)^{-1}A\) holds precisely when \(X_\star = Q + A^{\mathsf T}X_\star E\) — that is, when \(X_\star\) solves the DARE. So \(\operatorname{im}\begin{bmatrix}I\\X_\star\end{bmatrix}\) is the invariant subspace of \(S\) for the eigenvalues inside the unit disk. The fixed-point iteration is subspace iteration on \(S^{-1}\) with a particular normalization; \(2^k\) steps of it can be computed as \(\;S^{-2^k}\), if only you can square without wrecking things.

Here is the entire difficulty, and the entire idea. Squaring \(S^{-1}\) itself is numerically doomed: \(S\) has eigenvalues \(1/E\) outside the unit disk, so entries of \(S^{-2^k}\) grow like \(\|E\|^{-2^k}\) and swamp everything (for \(\|E\|=0.7\), that is \(10^{2.3}\) by \(k=8\), \(10^{300}\) by \(k=26\)). SDA instead maintains the factored representation

\[ S^{-2^k} \;=\; \begin{bmatrix} A_k & 0 \\ -Q_k & I \end{bmatrix}^{-1} \begin{bmatrix} I & G_k \\ 0 & A_k^{\mathsf T} \end{bmatrix}, \qquad (A_0,G_0,Q_0) = (A,G,Q), \]

and squares the representation. Multiplying two such factors gives a product of three block matrices; pushing it back into the standard form is one block Gaussian elimination — the Schur complement machinery of §2. Carrying it out (one-page of block algebra, or trust the machine-checked extraction in the demo script) yields the update with a single shared inverse \(T_k = (I + G_kQ_k)^{-1}\):

\[ A_{k+1} = A_k\,T_k\,A_k, \qquad G_{k+1} = G_k + A_k\,T_k\,G_k\,A_k^{\mathsf T}, \qquad Q_{k+1} = Q_k + A_k^{\mathsf T}Q_k\,T_k\,A_k. \]

These are exactly the three lines of §1. (Watch the placement in the last formula: \(Q_k\) sits between \(A_k^{\mathsf T}\) and \(T_k\). It is the placement that keeps \(Q_{k+1}\) symmetric, via the identity \((I+QG)^{-1}Q = Q(I+GQ)^{-1}\); the script’s diagnostic confirms it against the explicit matrix power \(S^{-2}\) to \(10^{-15}\).)

5.3 What the iteration preserves

This is where “structure-preserving” stops being a label and becomes a list:

  • The equation is invariant (the doubling lemma). For every \(k\), the DARE with data \((A_k, G_k, Q_k)\) has the same stabilizing solution \(X_\star\). Reason: powers of \(S\) do not change invariant subspaces, and the factorization is the equation. Verified: the residual of the true \(X_\star\) in the rewritten equations stays at \(10^{-14}\) for all \(k\).
  • Symmetry and positive semidefiniteness. Each increment is a congruence \(A_k(\cdot)A_k^{\mathsf T}\), so \(G_k,Q_k\succeq0\) and symmetric forever. Hence \(I + G_kQ_k\) — the only matrix ever inverted — has its spectrum in \([1,\infty)\) (eigenvalues of \(G_kQ_k\) equal those of the PSD matrix \(G_k^{1/2}Q_kG_k^{1/2}\)). The verified spectra on the test problem: real, from \(1.0000\) up to \(1.0145\). The algorithm cannot blow up through its own inverse.
  • Everything stays bounded; nothing explodes. In exact arithmetic \(A_k\to0\), \(G_k\to C_\star\) (the solution of the dual Riccati equation — it converges quadratically too, verified digits: 0.2, 1.0, 2.0, 4.2, 8.8, …) and \(Q_k\uparrow X_\star\) monotonically in the Löwner order, from below. Compare with the raw matrix \(S^{-2^k}\), whose entries overflow. You square the operator but store it in coordinates that stay finite.
  • \(Q_k\) is the \(2^k\)-th fixed-point iterate. Exact, not asymptotic: verified \(\|Q_2 - X_4\| = 3\times10^{-15}\). SDA is the same iteration with the steps teleported — which is why it wins: \(k\) cheap rewrites instead of \(2^k\) linear solves.

On the \(n=4\) test problem (\(\rho(A)=0.85\), closed-loop ratio \(\rho(E)=0.719\)):

6 · The bridge to continuous time: a Cayley transform

Everything so far lived in discrete time. The CARE’s natural object is the Hamiltonian matrix

\[ \mathcal H = \begin{bmatrix} A & -G \\ -Q & -A^{\mathsf T} \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \mathcal H \begin{bmatrix} I \\ X_\star\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} I \\ X_\star \end{bmatrix} (A - GX_\star), \]

whose spectrum is symmetric about the imaginary axis, with \(\operatorname{im}\big[\begin{smallmatrix}I\\X_\star\end{smallmatrix}\big]\) spanning the left-half-plane half. The doubling engine, however, is driven by the unit circle: it needs one half of the spectrum inside the disk and needs it to die like \(\rho^{2^k}\). Squaring \(\mathcal H\) directly is useless — both halves grow, and there is no fixed-point map to accelerate in the first place.

The bridge is a Cayley transform with a shift \(\tau > 0\). Consider the matrix pencil \((\mathcal H + \tau I,\; \mathcal H - \tau I)\): it has the same invariant subspaces as \(\mathcal H\) (its eigenvalues are \(\mu \mapsto \frac{\mu+\tau}{\tau-\mu}\)), and that map sends the entire open left half-plane inside the unit circle, because \(\operatorname{Re}\mu < 0 \iff |\mu + \tau| < |\mu - \tau|\). The imaginary axis becomes the unit circle:

Figure 2. The Cayley pencil \((\mathcal H+\tau I,\, \mathcal H-\tau I)\) keeps the invariant subspaces but moves the split from the imaginary axis to the unit circle. The stable half — the subspace holding \(X_\star\) — now dies like \(\rho^{2^k}\), which is exactly what the doubling engine consumes.

Concretely: the DARE-SDA of §5 applies verbatim once the Cayley pencil is put into the same factored form \(\big(\big[\begin{smallmatrix}A_0&0\\-Q_0&I\end{smallmatrix}\big], \big[\begin{smallmatrix}I&G_0\\0&A_0^{\mathsf T}\end{smallmatrix}\big]\big)\) — one block elimination (Schur complements again) on the pencil \((\mathcal H+\tau I, \mathcal H-\tau I)\). Carrying it out gives the initialization published by Chu, Fan & Lin (2005), with \(A_\tau = A - \tau I\):

\[ A_0 = I + 2\tau\,(A_\tau + G A_\tau^{-\mathsf T} Q)^{-1}, \qquad G_0 = 2\tau\,A_\tau^{-1} G\,(A_\tau^{\mathsf T} + Q A_\tau^{-1} G)^{-1}, \] \[ Q_0 = 2\tau\,(A_\tau^{\mathsf T} + Q A_\tau^{-1} G)^{-1}\, Q\, A_\tau^{-1}. \]

All three are symmetric, \(G_0, Q_0 \succeq 0\) when \(G,Q\succeq0\), and the DARE with data \((A_0,G_0,Q_0)\) has the same stabilizing solution \(X_\star\) as the CARE. On the \(n=4\) test problem (closed-loop eigenvalues \(-2.81\pm2.06j,\,-1.04,\,-0.43\)), verified:

  • DARE residual of the CARE solution: \(1.2\times10^{-13}\);
  • closed-loop ratio \(\rho\big((I+G_0X_\star)^{-1}A_0\big) = 0.6328\) — equal (to 4 digits) to the largest Cayley image \(\big|\frac{\mu+\tau}{\tau-\mu}\big|\) of the closed-loop eigenvalues: the transform moves every closed-loop mode to exactly where the doubling engine expects it;
  • \(\operatorname{spec}(S_{(A_0,G_0,Q_0)}) = \operatorname{spec}\big((\mathcal H-\tau I)^{-1}(\mathcal H+\tau I)\big)\) to \(2.6\times10^{-13}\): the pencil is faithfully represented.

6.1 Choosing τ

The shift is the one free knob, and it matters in both directions: \(\tau\) must not be an eigenvalue of \(A\) (the formulas contain \(A_\tau^{-1}\)), and the convergence ratio is the largest Cayley image of the closed-loop eigenvalues — so \(\tau \ll |\)eigenvalues\(|\) or \(\tau \gg |\)eigenvalues\(|\) pushes that ratio toward 1 and stalls the doubling. The formulas above give \(A_0 = -1,\; G_0 = 2,\; Q_0 = 2\), and the DARE \(x = 2 + \frac{x}{1+2x}\) has the same root — check it by hand. 6.3 The same thing as the matrix sign function

One more viewpoint, since you know the classical landscape: applying the Cayley map twice, \(\mu \mapsto \frac{\mu+\tau}{\tau-\mu}\) applied to \(\mathcal H_k\) with \(\mathcal H_{k+1} = \tfrac12\big(\mathcal H_k + \tau^2 \mathcal H_k^{-1}\big)\), is exactly the Newton matrix sign iteration — and the script verifies that the Cayley transform of \(\mathcal H_1\) equals \(S^2\) to \(10^{-10}\). SDA is the sign-function method with the invariant-subspace extraction at the end replaced by bookkeeping that carries \((A_k,G_k,Q_k)\) all the way through — which is precisely what lets it run in factored, low-rank form at scale.

Takeaway: the CARE reaches the doubling engine through a Cayley transform that moves the stable/unstable split onto the unit circle; one block elimination then produces the Chu–Fan–Lin starting triple, and the shift τ is the single tuning knob, with a flat optimum and hard singularities at \(\tau\in\operatorname{spec}(A)\).

7 · Why “structure-preserving” is the point

The classical Schur-vector method is backward stable, so why bother? Because it operates on a \(2n\times2n\) unstructured eigenproblem: the QR/QZ iteration does not know the eigenvalues come in ± pairs, so near the stability boundary tiny perturbations can split a pair across the wrong side, and \(X_\star = U_2U_1^{-1}\) inherits the conditioning of the invariant subspace. SDA never forms eigenvalues or eigenvectors. What it preserves, concretely:

  • symmetric, PSD iterates \(G_k, Q_k\) (congruence updates), hence a monotone, below-error \(Q_k \preceq X_\star\) and a well-posed monotone stopping test;
  • a single inverse per step, of \(I + G_kQ_k\), whose spectrum is provably in \([1,\infty)\) — the algorithm’s only dangerous operation is structurally harmless;
  • the symplectic/deflating structure in exact arithmetic, so no ± pairing can drift. (Honesty note: this preserves structure; it is not an all-purpose forward-stability theorem — those exist case by case in the literature.)

Cost: each step is one factorization of \(I+G_kQ_k\) plus a handful of \(n\times n\) products — cheap and vectorizable — and the quadratic rate buys machine precision in typically 6–15 steps total. Two practical superpowers follow. First, warm starts: re-solving with new weights in MPC or iteration-in-the-loop design can begin from the previous triple. Second, large scale: because \(G = BR^{-1}B^{\mathsf T}\) and \(Q = C^{\mathsf T}C\) are low-rank and \(A_k\) never needs to be formed explicitly (the updates can be rearranged with the Woodbury identity), the doubling iteration runs at \(O(n)\) per step on sparse problems — Li, Chu, Lin & Weng report an \(n = 20{,}209\) CARE with 204 million unknowns in \(X\) solved to \(10^{-16}\) in 45 seconds in MATLAB on a laptop.

Where it hurts: eigenvalues of \(\mathcal H\) on the imaginary axis (no unique stabilizing solution; in borderline cases the rate degrades from quadratic to linear with ratio ½), the \(\tau\) singularities of §6, unimodular eigenvalues for DAREs, and dense fill-in of \(A_k\) for sparse \(A\) unless the low-rank variants are used. Takeaway: structure preservation means the algorithm’s only inversion is spectrally trapped in \([1,\infty)\), its iterates stay in the PSD cone and bounded while the raw power iteration overflows — and the same pattern scales from a 5-line dense solver to \(O(n)\) large-scale solvers via low-rank factored updates.

8 · Summary and resources

The whole page in one breath: the Riccati fixed-point iteration is a power method on a symplectic matrix; squaring that matrix in its factored \((A_k,G_k,Q_k)\) representation — one Schur-complement bookkeeping per step — preserves the equation, the PSD cone, and boundedness while squaring the error; the CARE joins through a Cayley transform with shift τ. When you next type dare/icare or need the gain fast inside an inner loop, the doubling iteration is ~10 lines and gives you the answer with digits doubling every step.

Reproducibility: every table on this page (Stein, DARE, CARE, τ study, formula extraction) is produced by demos/sda_verify.py (numpy/scipy; run with uv run --with numpy --with scipy python demos/sda_verify.py), and demos/diag_sda.py documents how the update-formula placement was pinned down against the explicit matrix power \(S^{-2}\).
