The Structure-Preserving Doubling Algorithm and the Algebraic Riccati Equation

One growing mode, one quadratic matrix equation with several solutions, and one idea borrowed from repeated squaring: how the SDA turns an iteration whose error shrinks by a fixed factor into one whose digits double, using nothing but \(n\times n\) linear solves.

1. What must be computed, and with what?

Question: The optimal gain comes from a matrix equation that is quadratic in its unknown, so it has more than one solution, and the plant below has a growing mode. Which solution is the controller — and if the plant had a few thousand states, what would we even compute?

Take the plant and the cost

\[ \dot x=Ax+Bu,\qquad A=\begin{bmatrix}-1&0\\0&1\end{bmatrix},\qquad B=\begin{bmatrix}1\\1\end{bmatrix} \]
\[ J=\int_0^\infty\big(x^{\top}Qx+u^2\big)\,dt,\qquad Q=\operatorname{diag}(1,2) \]

The first state decays on its own; the second grows like \(e^{t}\). For this problem the optimal control is a static feedback \(u=-Kx\), and the classical result — used here, not re-derived — is that

\[ K=B^{\top}X,\qquad A^{\top}X+XA-XGX+Q=0,\qquad G=BB^{\top}, \]

where \(X=X^{\top}\succeq0\) is the solution that makes \(A-GX\) stable. The middle term \(XGX\) is quadratic in the unknown, so this continuous-time algebraic Riccati equation (CARE) is not a linear system: it has several symmetric solutions, and only the stabilizing one is the controller.

On the example it is \(X=\begin{bmatrix}1/2&-1/2\\-1/2&7/2\end{bmatrix}\), so \(K=B^{\top}X=\begin{bmatrix}0&3\end{bmatrix}\): the controller ignores the already-stable state and feeds the growing one back with gain \(3\). Substituting \(X\) into the CARE returns exactly zero in rational arithmetic (scripts/verify_sda_are.py, section A), and

\[ A-GX=\begin{bmatrix}-1&-3\\0&-2\end{bmatrix} \]

is stable: the pole at \(+1\) has moved to \(-2\), as Figure 1 shows.

Writing \(X\) down is one thing; computing it is another. The straight road builds the \(2n\times2n\) Hamiltonian matrix \(\begin{bmatrix}A&-G\\-Q&-A^{\top}\end{bmatrix}\) and reads \(X\) off its stable invariant subspace — the route taken by the QZ note for the discrete-time sibling of this equation. That costs an ordered Schur decomposition of a matrix twice the size of the plant. This note answers a different question: can we get \(X\) out of \(n\times n\) matrix inverses alone, with no eigenvalues and no Schur form — and how fast?

Takeaway: the controller is one particular symmetric solution of a quadratic matrix equation. Everything below is about computing that solution with \(n\times n\) linear algebra, and about how many such solves it takes.

2. The first attempt, and what it costs

Question: the Riccati equation is a fixed-point equation in disguise. Why not simply iterate it — or integrate the Riccati flow, which is how the finite-horizon problem is solved anyway?

Both obvious routes work. Both converge at a rate the problem chooses for us.

The flow. The finite-horizon Riccati differential equation \(\dot Y=Q+A^{\top}Y+YA-YGY\), started from \(Y(0)=0\), rises to \(X\) as the horizon grows. Integrating it on the running example with a classical RK4 step of \(dt=0.02\) gives the following accuracy against \(X\):

horizon \(t\)48121620
digits of \(Y(t)\)3.166.6310.1013.5814.88

The digits grow at about \(0.87\) per unit of time, which is the predicted rate: near the limit the error obeys \(\dot\Delta\approx(A-GX)^{\top}\Delta+\Delta(A-GX)\), so \(\|\Delta(t)\|\sim e^{-2t}\), set by the slowest closed-loop pole at \(-1\). Reaching a relative error of \(10^{-12}\) takes \(t\approx14.2\) — that is \(710\) RK4 steps and roughly \(2\,840\) evaluations of the Riccati map (script section E).

The sibling recursion. In discrete time the same equation reads \(X=Q+A^{\top}X(I+GX)^{-1}A\), and iterating it from \(X_0=0\),

\[ X_{j+1}=Q+A^{\top}X_j(I+GX_j)^{-1}A, \]

contracts the error by \(\sigma^2\) per step, where \(\sigma=\rho\big((I+GX)^{-1}A\big)<1\) — once again a fixed factor, once again dictated by the problem rather than by the algorithm.

The need, stated exactly: both iterations multiply the error by a constant below \(1\) at every step. We want a step that squares it: one that does the work of \(2^k\) plain steps in \(k\) steps, and that needs nothing beyond \(n\times n\) solves.
Takeaway: the obstacle is rate, not correctness. A constant contraction \(\rho<1\) buys \(O(1)\) digits per step no matter how large the plant is, and improving that constant is the whole game.

3. Doubling: do the steps in powers of two

Question: what is the smallest case in which “square the error” is not a hope but an identity we can write down?

Strip the Riccati equation of its nonlinear term. The Stein equation

\[ X=A^{\top}XA+Q,\qquad \rho(A)<1, \]

has the solution \(X=\sum_{i\ge0}(A^{\top})^iQA^i\), and iterating \(X_{j+1}=A^{\top}X_jA+Q\) from \(X_0=0\) produces exactly the partial sums \(X_j=\sum_{i<j}(A^{\top})^iQA^i\). Subtracting gives the error in closed form, \(X-X_j=(A^{\top})^jXA^j\): a constant factor per step and nothing more.

Now halve the remaining sum. The identity

\[ \sum_{i<2^{k+1}}(A^{\top})^iQA^i =\sum_{i<2^k}(A^{\top})^iQA^i +(A^{\top})^{2^k}\Big(\sum_{i<2^k}(A^{\top})^iQA^i\Big)A^{2^k} \]

says that the \(2^{k+1}\)-term partial sum is built from one \(2^k\)-term partial sum plus a copy of it conjugated by \(A_k=A^{2^k}\). Keeping only those two quantities gives the doubling

\[ A_{k+1}=A_k^2,\qquad Q_{k+1}=Q_k+A_k^{\top}Q_kA_k,\qquad A_0=A,\;Q_0=Q. \]
Claim (squaring a partial sum). For every \(k\), \(Q_k\) of the recursion above equals the plain iterate \(X_{2^k}\), and the remaining error is \(X-Q_k=(A_k^{\top})XA_k\).

Proof sketch: induction on \(k\), using the halving identity and \(A_k=A^{2^k}\). Nothing is approximated — \(k\) doubled steps reproduce the \(2^k\)-th plain step exactly.

That is the whole idea, and it is verifiable directly: on a \(2\times2\) Stein equation with \(\rho(A)=0.688\), the script compares \(Q_k\) with the explicit \(2^k\)-term sum for \(k\le6\) and finds a largest discrepancy of \(4.4\times10^{-16}\) (section G). The plain partial sums gain \(0.33\) digits per step; the doubled ones double that.

Putting the nonlinearity back looks like bad news. In the Riccati equation the matrix playing the role of \(A\) is not \(A\) at all — it is the closed-loop matrix \((I+GX)^{-1}A\), which depends on the unknown \(X\). The trick survives because the doubling can be written entirely in terms of the data:

\[ \begin{aligned} A_{k+1}&=A_k(I+G_kQ_k)^{-1}A_k,\\[2pt] G_{k+1}&=G_k+A_k(I+G_kQ_k)^{-1}G_kA_k^{\top},\\[2pt] Q_{k+1}&=Q_k+A_k^{\top}Q_k(I+G_kQ_k)^{-1}A_k, \end{aligned} \qquad A_0=A,\quad G_0=G,\quad Q_0=Q. \]

This is the structure-preserving doubling algorithm (SDA). It inherits the Stein identity: \(Q_k\) is again the \(2^k\)-th iterate of the plain recursion, \(A_k\to0\), \(Q_k\uparrow X\), and the multiplier \(\sigma_k=\rho\big((I+G_kX)^{-1}A_k\big)\) becomes \(\sigma^{2^k}\). The \(k=5\) entry is already at the float64 ceiling of about sixteen digits, where the last digit is arithmetic noise rather than accuracy.

Intuition: a fixed-point iteration walks to the answer one step at a time; a doubling algorithm jumps to the iterate with index \(2^k\) and carries just enough bookkeeping to take the next jump. The price is that the \(k\)-th problem is again a Riccati problem of the same shape — which is exactly why the bookkeeping is cheap.
Takeaway: “square the error” is literal: after \(k\) doublings the remaining contraction factor is \(\sigma^{2^k}\), so the digits double at every step — and none of the three lines mentions \(X\).

4. Why the update never needs to know \(X\)

Question: the three lines are written in terms of \(A_k,G_k,Q_k\) only. Why does the unknown \(X\) still solve the updated problem, and why is the new multiplier the square of the old one?

Rewriting the Riccati equation turns it into a statement about a subspace. If \(X\) is a solution, then

\[ \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}A&0\\-Q&I\end{bmatrix}}_{L} \begin{bmatrix}I\\X\end{bmatrix} =\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}I&G\\0&A^{\top}\end{bmatrix}}_{N} \begin{bmatrix}I\\X\end{bmatrix}K, \qquad K=(I+GX)^{-1}A. \]

So the block column \(\begin{bmatrix}I\\X\end{bmatrix}\) is a deflating subspace of the pencil \((L,N)\), and \(K\) is the operator that the pencil induces on it. Equivalently, \(\operatorname{Im}\begin{bmatrix}I\\X\end{bmatrix}\) is invariant under \(S=N^{-1}L\), with the eigenvalues of \(K\).

Two structural facts make this useful. First, \(S\) is symplectic when \(G=G^{\top}\) and \(Q=Q^{\top}\), so its eigenvalues come in reciprocal pairs \((\lambda,1/\bar\lambda)\). Under the standard hypotheses the \(n\) eigenvalues of \(K\) lie strictly inside the unit circle and the other \(n\) strictly outside, which is what “\(X\) stabilizes the closed loop” means in this language. Second, the plain iteration of §2 is (inverse) subspace iteration with \(S^{-1}\), which is why its rate is the ratio between the two halves of the spectrum.

Doubling now has a clean meaning: it computes \(S^{-2^k}\) directly. That is possible with a single block elimination because the factorization survives the power, with the same shape and new data,

\[ S^{-2^k} =\begin{bmatrix}A_k&0\\-Q_k&I\end{bmatrix}^{-1} \begin{bmatrix}I&G_k\\0&A_k^{\top}\end{bmatrix}, \]

where \(A_k,G_k,Q_k\) are the iterates of §3.

Claim. Let \(A_k,G_k,Q_k\) be defined by the three-line update from \((A,G,Q)\). Then (i) a matrix \(X\) solves the discrete Riccati equation with data \((A_k,G_k,Q_k)\) if and only if it solves the original one, and (ii) on that common deflating subspace the closed loops are related by \(K_k=(I+G_kX)^{-1}A_k=K^{2^k}\). Consequently \(\sigma_k=\sigma^{2^k}\) and \(A_k\to0\) whenever \(\sigma<1\).

Proof sketch: square the factorization, then eliminate the top-left block. The Schur complement that appears is \(I+G_kQ_k\), so one \(n\times n\) inverse produces the next three blocks; symplecticity forces the off-diagonal blocks to stay Hermitian, which is what closes the recursion on \((A_k,G_k,Q_k)\). Part (ii) follows by comparing \(S^{-2^k}\begin{bmatrix}I\\X\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}I\\X\end{bmatrix}K^{2^k}\) with the same product read from the factorization — and it is what the “measured” row of the §3 table checks. The general convergence theory (which hypotheses give \(\sigma<1\), and what happens at the boundary) is Lin and Xu's doubling transformation for symplectic pencils; see §7.

Takeaway: the three-line update is not a heuristic on the data. It is an exact rewrite of the Riccati problem that keeps the deflating subspace fixed and raises its spectrum to the \(2^k\)-th power.

5. The method, and the running example finished

Question: the doubling runs on a discrete-time Riccati equation, but our plant is continuous. How do we bridge the two, and what does the algorithm actually return?

Step 1 — bridge with a Cayley transform. Choose a shift \(\tau>0\) for which \(A_\tau:=A-\tau I\) is invertible, and set

\[ K_\tau=A_\tau^{\top}+QA_\tau^{-1}G,\qquad A_0=I+2\tau K_\tau^{-\top},\quad G_0=2\tau A_\tau^{-1}GK_\tau^{-1},\quad Q_0=2\tau K_\tau^{-1}QA_\tau^{-1}. \]

Then every solution of the CARE is a solution of the discrete Riccati equation with data \((A_0,G_0,Q_0)\), and stability is preserved: the closed-loop eigenvalues are carried by \(\lambda\mapsto c(\lambda)=\dfrac{\lambda+\tau}{\lambda-\tau}\), which maps the open left half-plane onto the open unit disk. Symmetry of \(G\) and \(Q\) is preserved too, so the SDA's structure survives the change of variables.

For the running example with \(\tau=2\), and in exact rational arithmetic,

\[ A_0=\begin{bmatrix}-2/7&6/7\\1/7&-3/7\end{bmatrix},\qquad G_0=\begin{bmatrix}1/7&3/7\\3/7&9/7\end{bmatrix},\qquad Q_0=\begin{bmatrix}3/7&-2/7\\-2/7&20/7\end{bmatrix}, \]

whose closed loop is exactly \(\begin{bmatrix}-1/3&1\\0&0\end{bmatrix}\) — eigenvalues \(-1/3\) and \(0\), so \(\sigma=1/3\). That is the number behind every row of the §3 table.

Step 2 — double until the residual stops moving. The whole algorithm:

Ak, Gk, Qk = A, G, Q
for k in range(8):
    W  = inv(I + Gk @ Qk)                   # the only inverse in a step
    Ak1 = Ak @ W @ Ak
    Gk1 = Gk + Ak @ W @ Gk @ Ak.T
    Qk1 = Qk + Ak.T @ Qk @ W @ Ak           # Q before W is what keeps Qk symmetric
    Ak, Gk, Qk = Ak1, Gk1, Qk1
X = Qk                                      # the stabilizing solution
# the dual solution, if wanted, is Gk

Step 3 — read the answer off, and check it. \(X=\lim Q_k\), and the residual of the Riccati equation at the returned matrix is the stopping test. On the running example the SDA reaches \(10^{-12}\) in \(4\) steps; the plain fixed-point iteration of §2 needs \(13\), and the Riccati flow needs \(710\) RK4 steps — about \(2\,840\) evaluations of the Riccati map — for the same accuracy. The returned matrix agrees with the \(\begin{bmatrix}1/2&-1/2\\-1/2&7/2\end{bmatrix}\) of §1 to \(4.4\times10^{-15}\) in Frobenius norm after those four steps, and the Riccati residual there is \(8.6\times10^{-15}\); at the exact \(X\) the same residual is \(1.9\times10^{-16}\) in float64 and exactly zero in rational arithmetic.

Takeaway: a Cayley transform turns the CARE into a discrete-time Riccati equation with the same solution, and four or five doublings then solve it to working precision: the running example's \(X\) arrives with the digits of Figure 3.

What it costs. Each step needs one \(n\times n\) inverse and a handful of \(n\times n\) products — \(O(n^3)\) work per step, a small constant, and no eigensolver, no ordered Schur form, and no stabilizing initial guess. That last point separates the method from Newton's, which is also quadratically convergent but starts from a stabilizing \(K\) and solves a Lyapunov equation per step. In practice \(5\) to \(8\) steps reach working precision.

6. Where the guarantee stops

Question: change one knob — the shift \(\tau\) — and then one hypothesis, \(\sigma<1\). What breaks first, and what does the method degrade into?

The shift is a real design choice. Same plant, same algorithm, different \(\tau\):

\(\tau\)0.250.51\(\sqrt2\)2481632
\(\sigma(\tau)\)0.7780.600—0.1720.3330.6000.7780.8820.939
SDA steps to \(10^{-12}\)65—445678
plain steps to \(10^{-12}\)5829—9132857114227

At \(\tau=1\) the bridge does not merely perform badly; it does not exist, because \(A\) has an eigenvalue at \(+1\) and \(A-\tau I\) is singular. Both extremes are wasteful, and the interior optimum balances the two closed-loop multipliers: here it is \(\tau=\sqrt2\), where they are equal and \(\sigma=3-2\sqrt2\approx0.1716\). Chu, Fan and Lin suggest a golden-section search for the shift, and it is a genuine tuning knob rather than a formality.

The critical case. If the closed loop has an eigenvalue on the imaginary axis, the transformed problem has a multiplier of modulus \(1\), and squaring cannot push it anywhere. The mechanism is visible in the smallest instance: for a \(2\times2\) Jordan block with \(|\lambda|=1\),

\[ J=\begin{bmatrix}\lambda&1\\0&\lambda\end{bmatrix} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad J^{2^k}=\begin{bmatrix}\lambda^{2^k}&2^k\lambda^{2^k-1}\\0&\lambda^{2^k}\end{bmatrix}, \qquad \|J^{2^k}\|_F\approx2^k . \]

Measured, that norm runs \(1.73,2.45,4.24,8.12,16.06,\dots\) against \(1,2,4,8,16,\dots\) (script section H). A normalized doubling iteration therefore gains a constant number of bits per step instead of doubling them: for Riccati equations, convergence in the critical case is only linear, with rate \(1/2\) (Chiang, Chu, Guo, Huang, Lin and Xu).

Breakdown, and the role of the hypotheses. Every step must invert \(I+G_kQ_k\), and that matrix can become singular or badly conditioned along the way; modified SDAs that carry the symplectic pairing in a parametrized form are designed to avoid exactly this (Huang, Kuo, Lin and Shieh). The signs matter too: \(Q\succeq0\) and \(G\succeq0\) are not decoration. With an indefinite \(Q\) there are examples with no stabilizing solution at all and a symplectic matrix carrying unit-circle eigenvalues with odd Jordan blocks — outside the even-block case, the theory above has nothing to say.

Alternatives, and when they win. The ordered Schur and QZ route of the sibling note is direct and backward stable in one shot, at the price of a \(2n\times2n\) pencil and a reordering step. Newton's method is quadratically convergent but needs a stabilizing start. For large, sparse problems whose solution has low rank, a decoupled SDA with truncation keeps the cost per step near \(O(n)\). The SDA's own niche is the middle: dense or moderate-sized problems where a handful of \(n\times n\) solves and no eigensolver is exactly the trade you want.

Takeaway: the doubling is quadratic only while every closed-loop multiplier stays strictly inside the unit disk and \(I+G_kQ_k\) stays invertible. The shift has to be chosen, and both failure modes announce themselves in the numbers before the answer does.
